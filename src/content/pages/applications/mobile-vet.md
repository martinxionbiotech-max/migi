# Mobile and House-Call Practice: Running POCT Where the Environment Does Not Cooperate

**Quick Answer** — A mobile practice can run quantitative POCT reliably, but the constraint is not the instrument — it is the environment around it. Temperature, power, bench space and sample transport all vary between calls, and an assay whose kinetics are temperature-dependent will drift with them. The workable approach is to control the analyzer's local environment, keep a mobile-specific QC rhythm, and be explicit about which samples cannot be processed on site.

## Quick Answer

Clinic protocols assume a controlled room. Mobile protocols must assume the opposite and compensate deliberately.

## The four environmental constraints

| Constraint | What goes wrong | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Kinetics shift; results drift; cartridges equilibrate slowly | Insulated case, avoid direct sun, allow equilibration time before running |
| Power | Interrupted runs, incomplete reads | Battery/inverter sizing based on peak draw; charge discipline |
| Vibration and movement | Pipetting error, cartridge misalignment | Park before running; fixed bench surface inside the vehicle |
| Space and light | Hurried technique, misread labels | A defined working tray; task lighting |

## Sample handling away from the clinic

The decisive question is whether the assay's validated sample type requires separation. If it does:

1. **Carry a centrifuge** sized to the sample volume, or
2. **Return the sample** to the clinic within the platform's stability window, and
3. **Document the delay** in the record — a value with an unrecorded transport time cannot be interpreted later.

Whole-blood-capable assays are a genuine advantage in this setting; serum-based assays are not, because clotting time competes with the visit.

## QC that survives a mobile schedule

A clinic QC schedule assumes the analyzer sits still in a controlled room. Mobile practice needs a different rhythm:

- **Controls at the start and end of each mobile session**, not once a day.
- **A control after any temperature excursion** — a van that sat in the sun for two hours is an excursion.
- **A control cartridges carried with the analyzer**, stored under the same conditions, so the control reflects the instrument's actual environment.
- **A written log in the vehicle**, because the clinic record is not present at the point of use.

The failure mode to design against is the one where controls are run weekly "because there is no time", and a drift is only noticed when a result contradicts the clinical picture.

## Which tests earn a place on a house call

| Test | Mobile suitability | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Parvovirus antigen | High | Faecal/rectal sample, no separation, immediate decision |
| Inflammatory markers | Medium | Needs plasma separation in most formats |
| Progesterone | Medium–high | Timing-critical, but sample type dependent |
| Cardiac markers | Low–medium | Sample handling sensitivity; often better sent |
| Renal panel | Low | Pre-analytical sensitivity, and rarely changes an on-site decision |

## Six traps

1. **Assuming the vehicle's climate control is the analyzer's climate control.** It is not.
2. **Running a cartridge straight from a cold case.** Equilibration time is not optional.
3. **Weekly controls on a daily-use device.** Drift goes undetected.
4. **Processing a sample whose validated type needs separation.** The result is not interpretable.
5. **No excursion protocol.** A hot afternoon becomes an unexplained anomaly.
6. **Promoting a broad menu for mobile use.** Only a few assays genuinely suit it.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** The mobile segment is small but strategically valuable: it is where a platform's practical design either shows or does not, and the vendors who support it properly earn unusual loyalty.

**Reasoning.** Mobile practitioners cannot absorb complexity — they work alone, under time pressure, with no bench and no colleague to check a decision. A platform that publishes a mobile protocol, a short menu that actually works on the road, and a control rhythm designed for vehicles is solving a problem the clinician feels every day. That is a different kind of loyalty from the one bought with a discount, and it travels: mobile vets talk to each other in narrow networks, and a tool that works in a van gets recommended with more conviction than one that works in a well-equipped clinic.
