# Animal Shelters: Outbreak Control on a Fixed Budget

**Quick Answer** — A shelter does not have a diagnostic problem; it has a **movement problem**. Every animal that enters, leaves, or moves between wards is a transmission opportunity, and testing only earns its cost when it changes one of those movements. That is why the useful shelter strategy is risk-based testing plus cohorting, not universal screening: a test that does not change an action is a donation to the virus.

## Quick Answer

Test to decide, not to know. The three decisions that matter are: isolate or not, treat or not, move or not.

## Intake strategy

| Animal | Action | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Symptomatic (diarrhoea, vomiting, lethargy) | Test immediately, isolate pending result | Highest yield; protects the population |
| Known exposure (littermate, same transport) | Test, cohort separately | Incubation means today's negative is provisional |
| Healthy adult, vaccinated | No antigen test; vaccinate and house | Low yield; consumes budget |
| Healthy puppy/kitten, unknown history | Vaccinate, observe, test only if signs develop | Highest risk group — but the intervention is vaccination, not testing |
| Pre-adoption / pre-transfer | Test if destination requires it | A movement decision, not a diagnosis |

## The response timeline

**Day 0 — confirm and isolate.** Test the index case, isolate immediately, and stop movement between areas. Do not wait for a second case to confirm the pattern.

**Day 1 — cohort and vaccinate.** Split the population into exposed and unexposed. Vaccinate the susceptible population — vaccination is the intervention with the largest effect per unit cost, and it acts faster than testing can.

**Day 3 — retest the exposed cohort.** A negative on day 0 does not clear an animal: incubation means animals can be pre-symptomatic and non-shedding at first test.

**Day 7 — review and release.** Relax restrictions only after a full incubation window with no new cases. Releasing early is how outbreaks get a second wave.

## Cohorting rules that actually hold

1. **Physical separation, not just labelled kennels.** Shared corridors and staff hands move virus.
2. **Staff assignment:** one person per cohort where staffing allows; the clean cohort handled first.
3. **Separate equipment** — cleaning tools, bowls, scales.
4. **Movement log** — every animal movement recorded with date and cohort, so a new case can be traced.
5. **Foster and transfer holds** during the response window.

## Budget allocation under constraint

A shelter with a fixed testing budget faces a genuine triage problem. The defensible order:

1. Confirm and characterise the index case (one or two tests).
2. Test symptomatic animals in the exposed cohort (changes isolation).
3. Test cohort contacts at day 3 (changes release timing).
4. Asymptomatic screening (changes little).
5. Pre-transfer testing for destinations that require it (paid by the destination where possible).

Cost per case prevented is the metric, not tests performed. A shelter that tests 200 asymptomatic animals and misses two incubating ones in a cohort has spent money and bought nothing.

## The hard decisions

Shelter medicine involves capacity limits that private practice does not: isolation space, staffing, and the risk of a ward-wide outbreak. Testing supports those decisions with evidence — but the decision itself remains a welfare and resource judgement, not a laboratory one. Written protocols, agreed in advance, reduce the moral weight carried by individual staff members at 2 a.m.

## Six traps

1. **Universal antigen screening.** Low yield, high cost, and it feels like control without providing it.
2. **Clearing animals on a day-0 negative.** Incubation window ignored.
3. **Vaccinating after the outbreak is established.** Earlier is dramatically more effective.
4. **Shared equipment between cohorts.** The most common breach.
5. **Releasing restrictions at day 3.** Second waves are usual when this happens.
6. **Testing without a movement consequence.** Data with no action attached.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** For shelters, the vendor's obligation is to publish a protocol, not to sell more tests — and the vendors who do will own this segment.

**Reasoning.** Shelter budgets are fixed and scrutiny is high, so a vendor that recommends universal screening is recommending something the shelter cannot sustain, and the relationship ends when the budget is cut. A vendor that publishes a risk-based protocol with a timeline becomes the reference for shelter outbreak management, which is a durable position: shelters talk to each other, and a protocol that works gets copied across a region. The commercial logic is not altruism — it is that a modest amount of testing used correctly produces better outcomes and better references than a large amount used indiscriminately.
