# Infectious Disease Testing: Choosing the Right Test for the Sign

**Quick Answer** — Most infectious disease testing errors are not analytical; they are categorical. A clinician orders the wrong *kind* of test (antibody where antigen is needed), at the wrong *time* (outside the shedding window), on the wrong *sample* (a nasal swab where a conjunctival one is needed). This page is organised around the decision, not around the pathogen.

## Quick Answer

Antigen for the sick animal today. Antibody for the immune question tomorrow. Everything else is timing and sample quality.

## The categorical distinction

| | Antigen | Antibody |
|---|---|---|
| Question | Is the pathogen present now? | Has this animal met it? |
| Use in acute disease | Primary | Supplementary (unvaccinated animals) |
| Effect of vaccination | None | Confounds interpretation completely |
| Window | During shedding — often narrow | Weeks after exposure; persists |
| Typical false result | Negative (early, late, poor sample, hook effect) | Positive (vaccination) |

In practice, this table prevents the two dominant errors: diagnosing infection from a vaccinated animal's antibody, and excluding disease on a single antigen test taken at the wrong moment.

## By clinical sign

**Haemorrhagic diarrhoea, puppy.** Parvovirus antigen first. Add coronavirus antigen where the animal is severe or co-infection is suspected. Consider that a positive coronavirus result in an unwell puppy often accompanies parvovirus rather than replacing it.

**Haemorrhagic diarrhoea, kitten.** Feline panleukopenia antigen. Note that parvovirus assays may cross-detect FPV and CPV, which is useful but must be interpreted with the species in mind.

**Upper respiratory signs, cat.** Calicivirus and herpesvirus antigen from oropharyngeal/conjunctival swabs. Herpesvirus is a latency virus — a negative between episodes is expected, and recurrent ocular disease should raise suspicion regardless of the swab result.

**Neurological signs, dog.** Distemper antigen, with the caveat that antigen may be undetectable at the neurological stage. Antibody is not diagnostic in a vaccinated dog.

**Effusion or fever of unknown origin, young cat.** Feline coronavirus — but on effusion, not on serum antibody. An antibody titre does not diagnose FIP.

**Weight loss, anaemia, recurrent infection, cat.** Feline leukaemia virus antigen and feline immunodeficiency virus antibody, as a pair. Two retroviruses, one sample.

**Weight loss, skin lesions, endemic region.** Leishmania antibody, interpreted with species, endemicity and clinical signs.

## Building a co-infection panel

A panel is justified when the result changes management beyond what a single test would. Four patterns cover most of the value:

| Setting | Panel | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Shelter intake, diarrhoea | Parvovirus + coronavirus | Co-infection alters prognosis and isolation |
| Cattery, respiratory outbreak | Calicivirus + herpesvirus | Management differs (antiviral vs supportive) |
| Sick kitten | Panleukopenia + FeLV + FIV | Retrovirus status changes long-term plan |
| Breeding group | Progesterone timing + (later) relaxin | Two different questions, sequenced |

Panels that only confirm an already-made diagnosis add cost without changing action.

## Why a negative result is not the end

The four mechanisms behind a false negative deserve equal weight in training:

1. **Timing** — before shedding or after it has declined.
2. **Sample** — wrong site, poor technique, insufficient material.
3. **Antigen excess** — very high concentrations read low; dilution confirms.
4. **Interference** — haemolysis, lipaemia, or a heavily contaminated sample.

The practical rule: in a strongly suspected case, a negative result is a prompt to repeat with a better sample, not a conclusion.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** An index page organised by clinical sign will outperform one organised by pathogen name, because clinicians arrive with a sign and a patient, not with a taxonomy.

**Reasoning.** Pathogen-organised menus are convenient for manufacturers and useless at the point of care: nobody wakes up wanting to test for a virus, they want to know why the puppy has bloody diarrhoea. A sign-first index also does something a pathogen list cannot — it makes the trade-offs visible, including when not to test, which is where most of the cost and most of the harm sits. For the vendor, the practical benefit is that such a page becomes the page clinicians bookmark, and a bookmarked page is worth more than ten pages that rank once and are never returned to.
