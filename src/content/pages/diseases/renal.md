# Renal Testing: Three Markers, One Question, and What Each Cannot Answer

**Quick Answer** — All three renal markers answer the same narrow question: how well is the glomerulus filtering? None of them answers the questions that most change treatment — is there protein loss, can the kidney concentrate urine, is there infection or tubular damage? A renal workup that consists only of filtration markers is therefore incomplete by design, whatever the marker's analytical quality.

## Quick Answer

Markers disagree because they measure different things imperfectly. The disagreement is information, not noise.

## The three markers

| Marker | Strength | Limitation | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Creatinine | Universal, well understood, cheap | Muscle-mass dependent; insensitive until substantial loss | Baseline and staging |
| SDMA | Less muscle-mass dependent | Not a damage marker; still confounded by hydration | Discordant cases; wasted or muscular patients |
| Cystatin C | Orthogonal to creatinine | Thinner veterinary evidence base; non-renal influences | Third opinion when the first two disagree |

## Reading the disagreement

| Creatinine | SDMA | Likely explanation | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | High | Reduced filtration likely | Rehydrate, urinalysis, blood pressure |
| High | Normal | Muscle mass effect or prerenal azotaemia | Recheck after rehydration |
| Normal | High | Early filtration loss or muscle-wasting effect | Repeat both on one sample; assess proteinuria |
| Normal | Normal | Filtration likely preserved | Still check urine: protein, sediment, specific gravity |

The third row is the clinically interesting one: it is where an early decline can first appear, and where repeating on a single sample matters more than adding a fourth marker.

## What the markers do not tell you

| Question | Marker that answers it |
|---|---|
| Is there protein loss? | Urine protein:creatinine ratio |
| Can the kidney concentrate? | Urine specific gravity |
| Is there infection or inflammation? | Urinalysis, sediment, culture |
| Is the damage tubular or glomerular? | Urinalysis, protein pattern, biopsy |
| Is blood pressure elevated? | Blood pressure measurement |

A cat with a normal SDMA and a UPC of 3.0 has active disease and needs treatment, regardless of its filtration markers. This is the single most important sentence for anyone building a renal protocol.

## Structuring the workup

1. **Creatinine and urine specific gravity together.** Neither alone separates prerenal from renal azotaemia.
2. **SDMA where the creatinine picture is confounded** — muscular breeds, severe muscle wasting, or a discrepancy with clinical signs.
3. **UPC** in every animal with suspected or confirmed renal disease, because proteinuria is both a staging variable and a treatment target.
4. **Blood pressure**, because hypertension both causes and results from renal injury.
5. **Staging** where chronic kidney disease is confirmed, using the current guideline framework.

## Monitoring

For a stable patient, the useful monitoring set is creatinine or SDMA (consistently the same marker), UPC, blood pressure, body weight and clinical status. Changing markers mid-course produces a trend that cannot be interpreted; the same-analyzer principle applies across the whole panel, not just to one assay.

## Six traps

1. **Treating SDMA as a replacement for creatinine.** They answer related but different questions.
2. **Skipping urinalysis.** The most consequential omission in renal workups.
3. **Diagnosing renal disease in a dehydrated animal.** Repeat after rehydration.
4. **Ignoring muscle mass.** Both directions: muscular dogs and wasted cats.
5. **Comparing markers across platforms.** Calibration differs.
6. **Monitoring with different markers on different visits.** Trend becomes uninterpretable.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** Renal is the category where a vendor should compete on the **completeness of the workup**, not on the sophistication of one marker.

**Reasoning.** The market conversation has been about which filtration marker is better, and that conversation has largely run its course — clinicians know that both creatinine and SDMA have limits. What remains unsolved is the practical one: most renal workups still skip UPC and blood pressure, which are the variables that actually change treatment. A vendor that publishes a workup sequence with a clear "these markers do not answer these questions" table is doing something more valuable than claiming superiority for its assay, and it positions the platform as the entry point to a complete assessment rather than a single-number screen.
