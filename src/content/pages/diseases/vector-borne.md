# Vector-Borne and Heartworm Testing: The Clinical Need, and Where a POCT Menu Ends

**Quick Answer** — Heartworm and tick-borne disease are among the most frequently tested conditions in small animal practice, and each requires a different assay format: antigen for heartworm status, serology for exposure to tick-borne agents, and nucleic-acid testing where active infection must be distinguished from exposure. A point-of-care menu that covers part of this list should say so plainly, because a clinician who assumes coverage that does not exist will miss the diagnosis.

## Quick Answer

The clinical need is real and routine. Whether a given platform covers it is a separate question, and the answer should be visible before the test is ordered.

## What each format detects

| Agent | Format | Detects | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dirofilaria immitis (heartworm) | Antigen | Adult female worm protein — current infection | Negative before adults develop; occult infections |
| Anaplasma spp. | Antibody / PCR | Exposure (serology) or active infection (PCR) | Serology stays positive after clearance |
| Ehrlichia spp. | Antibody / PCR | As above | Cross-reactivity within the genus; chronic phases |
| Borrelia burgdorferi | Antibody (with confirmation) | Exposure | Vaccination confounds some assays; exposure ≠ disease |
| Babesia spp. | PCR / cytology | Active infection | Serology less useful; organism detection is the goal |

The organising principle: **antigen answers "infected now", antibody answers "exposed at some point", PCR answers "organism present now at detectable levels"**. Confusing these produces the classic errors — treating a seropositive, clinically normal dog for an infection it has cleared, or excluding heartworm on a test taken too early.

## Heartworm: timing dominates interpretation

Antigen detection requires adult female worms, which means a window of months between infection and a positive result. Guidelines handle this with defined testing intervals tied to prevention status, not with a single test at any convenient time.

Practical implications:

1. **A negative result in a dog with no prevention history is provisional** until the recommended interval has elapsed.
2. **Antigen testing is the status test**; antibody testing does not establish infection status and is not a substitute.
3. **Microfilaria testing answers a different question** — whether the dog is a source for mosquitoes.
4. **Treatment decisions depend on staging**, which includes imaging and clinical assessment, not on the antigen result alone.

## Geography and seasonality

Prevalence varies enormously by region, and a test's predictive value changes with it: in a low-prevalence area, a positive result on a screening test has a higher chance of being false positive, which is why confirmation matters more there. A page that ignores geography teaches clinicians to interpret every result as if it came from an endemic region.

## Building a workable strategy

1. **Prevention-first.** Testing supports prevention; it does not replace it.
2. **Use the format that matches the question.** Antigen for infection status, serology for exposure, PCR where active infection must be proven.
3. **Confirm positives in low-prevalence settings.** Predictive value changes with prevalence.
4. **Document what was sent out.** A patient record that says "vector panel — all negative" without listing the agents is not auditable.
5. **Repeat at guideline intervals** where exposure risk continues.

## Six traps

1. **Assuming a panel covers every agent.** The most consequential assumption in this area.
2. **Excluding heartworm on an early negative.** Timing window ignored.
3. **Treating seropositive healthy dogs.** Exposure is not disease.
4. **Ignoring vaccination status in Borrelia interpretation.** Assay-dependent.
5. **Reading a genus-level cross-reaction as a species diagnosis.** Anaplasma and Ehrlichia assays vary.
6. **Skipping confirmation in low-prevalence regions.** Predictive value is prevalence-dependent.
