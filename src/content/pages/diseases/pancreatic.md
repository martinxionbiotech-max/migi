# Pancreatic Testing: A Composite Diagnosis, Not a Number

**Quick Answer** — Pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity is the best single laboratory test for pancreatitis, and it is still not a diagnosis. The disease is defined by a combination of compatible clinical signs, an elevated pancreas-specific lipase, and supporting imaging or laboratory evidence. Everything that goes wrong with this test goes wrong at the boundary between "elevated" and "diagnosed".

## Quick Answer

The test measures enzyme leakage, not disease severity. Use it to support a composite, not to replace one.

## What the assay adds

| Comparison | Catalytic lipase activity | Pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity |
|---|---|---|
| Specificity for pancreas | Low — multiple sources | High — pancreatic protein |
| Affected by renal clearance | Yes | Less so |
| Use in cats | Particularly unreliable | Preferred |
| Interpretation | Poorly standardised across labs | Assay-specific thresholds |

The move from activity to immunoreactivity is the single biggest improvement in this area, and it is why the test deserves its own page rather than a paragraph on a general chemistry panel.

## Where it changes management

1. **Confirming a clinical suspicion** in a dog with vomiting, cranial abdominal pain and an equivocal ultrasound.
2. **Resolving discordance** — a high value with a normal pancreas, or a normal value with an abnormal one, both teach something.
3. **Supporting a decision to treat intensively** rather than send home, when signs are mild but the value is markedly elevated.
4. **Feline cases**, where clinical signs are often non-specific and imaging can be inconclusive.

## Where it does not

- **Severity assessment.** The magnitude does not reliably predict outcome; organ dysfunction and clinical status do.
- **Excluding disease.** Mild, chronic and focal pancreatitis can sit inside the interval.
- **Stand-alone diagnosis.** Especially in cats, and especially with concurrent disease.
- **Rapid monitoring.** Values lag clinical improvement by days.

## The feline complication

In cats, pancreatitis is commonly part of a triad with cholangitis and inflammatory bowel disease. A modestly elevated fPL in that setting may reflect shared inflammation rather than a primary pancreatic event, and treating it as primary pancreatitis leads to an incomplete plan.

The useful pairing is fPL with an acute-phase protein:

| fPL | SAA | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| High | High | Pancreatitis with systemic response |
| High | Normal | Pancreatic involvement, no systemic response |
| Normal | High | Inflammation elsewhere — biliary, intestinal, urinary |

## Six traps

1. **Diagnosing on a single elevated value.** The composite is the diagnosis.
2. **Excluding pancreatitis on a normal value.** Chronic and focal forms exist.
3. **Using the number as a severity score.** Clinical status decides severity.
4. **Importing thresholds.** Systematic misclassification.
5. **Expecting rapid normalisation.** Values lag improvement.
6. **Ignoring concurrent disease in cats.** Triaditis changes the plan.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** The commercially useful promise is not "detects pancreatitis" but "resolves the case where you are unsure" — and that promise is kept by publishing a decision table, not by claiming sensitivity.

**Reasoning.** Every vendor claims detection. What clinicians cannot buy anywhere is help with the ambiguous case: the mildly elevated value in a well-looking dog, the normal value in a cat that clearly has something wrong. Those cases consume clinical time and generate repeat visits, and a decision table shortens them at zero experimental cost. It also has a defensive function: the most common complaint about a pancreatic assay is that it "was wrong" in a case where the interpretation, not the assay, was the problem. Publishing the table pre-empts that complaint by making the interpretation the clinic's stated protocol.
