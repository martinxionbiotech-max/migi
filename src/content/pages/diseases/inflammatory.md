# Inflammatory Disease Testing: Reading Magnitude, Not Just Presence

**Quick Answer** — Acute-phase proteins answer "how much systemic inflammation is there, and is it changing". They do not answer "where" or "from what". The clinical value comes from three uses: detecting a response that the examination missed, grading its severity, and tracking the response to treatment. A single value used as a diagnosis is the most common way these assays are wasted.

## Quick Answer

Magnitude first, trend second, location never.

## Species first, marker second

| Species | Dominant acute-phase protein | Practical implication |
|---|---|---|
| Dog | C-reactive protein (CRP) | CRP is the primary marker; interpret against a canine interval |
| Cat | Serum amyloid A (SAA) | SAA carries the response; CRP is a weaker signal in cats |

This is the single most consequential species difference in the inflammatory menu, and it is routinely ignored when clinics use one marker across both species.

## What a value tells you, in three layers

**Layer 1 — is there a systemic response?** A value clearly above the interval says yes, at the time of sampling. A value inside the interval says no *large* response; it does not exclude localized disease.

**Layer 2 — how severe?** Magnitude correlates with the scale of the inflammatory process, which supports triage: a modest elevation in an ambulatory animal and a marked elevation in a collapsed one describe different clinical states, and the number is one input among several.

**Layer 3 — is it resolving?** This is where these assays earn their price. A falling value after appropriate therapy is evidence that the intervention worked; a flat or rising value in a clinically improving animal suggests an ongoing or second process.

## Where the tests change management

| Scenario | Contribution |
|---|---|
| Pyrexia of unknown origin | A normal value redirects the differential away from systemic inflammation |
| Post-operative monitoring | A value that fails to fall suggests complication earlier than clinical signs |
| Sepsis suspicion | Magnitude supports triage; serial values support response assessment |
| Chronic enteropathy / cholangitis (cat) | SAA tracks the inflammatory component and responds to therapy |
| Monitoring immunosuppressive therapy | Trend supports dose decisions alongside clinical response |
| Pre-anaesthetic screening | Low yield unless a specific question exists |

The last row is a caution: routine pre-anaesthetic acute-phase testing rarely changes the anaesthetic plan.

## Pairing markers across species

Where both are available, the feline pair (SAA with fPL) is more informative than either alone:

- **High SAA, high fPL** — pancreatitis with a systemic response; manage accordingly.
- **High SAA, normal fPL** — inflammation elsewhere; look at biliary tree, intestine, urinary tract.
- **Normal SAA, high fPL** — pancreatic involvement without systemic response.

## What the assays do not do

- **They do not localize.** No acute-phase protein tells you which organ.
- **They do not identify an organism.** They are not a substitute for culture or cytology.
- **They do not distinguish infectious from sterile inflammation.** A sterile abscess and a bacterial infection can produce the same magnitude.
- **They do not replace serial clinical assessment.** A trend in a patient that is clinically unchanged warrants a second look at the trend, not an immediate escalation based on the number alone.

## Six traps

1. **Using CRP as the feline marker.** Understates the response in cats.
2. **Treating a single value as a diagnosis.** It is a magnitude.
3. **Excluding disease on a normal value.** Localized and chronic processes hide inside the interval.
4. **Changing platform mid-monitoring.** Bias shift mimics clinical change.
5. **Ignoring the sampling interval.** A re-check drawn too early sits inside assay noise.
6. **Acting on a trend that is smaller than the assay's imprecision.** The number moved; the patient did not.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** The inflammatory menu's real product is a **monitoring loop**, and a vendor should sell it that way — paired baseline and follow-up results, not single tests.

**Reasoning.** A single CRP in an emergency case is often redundant with the clinical picture; the value emerges when the same patient is measured again after 48 hours of therapy. Vendors that market acute-phase proteins as diagnostic tests compete with clinical judgement and lose. Vendors that market them as a follow-up instrument are selling something no examination can provide: an objective measure of whether the treatment is working. That framing also aligns the commercial incentive with good practice, because it makes repeat testing the intended use rather than an upsell.
