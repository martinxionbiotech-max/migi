# Cardiac Testing: Ruling Out, Not Screening In

**Quick Answer** — The cardiac biomarker earns its place in one specific moment: a dyspnoeic animal where the clinician must decide between cardiac and respiratory causes before imaging is available. In that setting, a low value meaningfully reduces the probability of heart failure. In an asymptomatic animal with a murmur, the same test produces mostly ambiguity and unnecessary referrals.

## Quick Answer

Use it to exclude, not to discover.

## The value of a negative

Diagnostic tests are usually discussed in terms of their positives. For NT-proBNP the more useful property is the negative:

| Result | Dyspnoeic cat or dog | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Clearly low | Any | Heart failure unlikely; investigate respiratory causes |
| Intermediate | Any | Grey zone; imaging needed — do not treat on the number |
| Clearly high | Compatible signs | Heart failure plausible; but check renal function and age first |

A low value in a struggling animal is actionable within minutes: oxygen, cage rest, and a respiratory workup rather than diuretics. That is a decision the point-of-care platform can support before radiography or echocardiography is available.

## The confounders that produce most discordance

| Confounder | Effect | Handling |
|---|---|---|
| Renal disease | Reduced clearance raises values | Interpret with creatinine/SDMA |
| Age | Values rise in some populations | Use age-appropriate interval |
| Pulmonary hypertension | Ventricular strain | Consider right-sided assessment |
| Sepsis / systemic inflammation | Can raise values | Interpret with the inflammatory picture |
| Severe respiratory distress | Physiology changes rapidly | Repeat after stabilisation where feasible |

The renal interaction explains most misleading results in cats, who frequently have both cardiac and renal disease.

## Where the test does not help

- **Asymptomatic screening.** Low yield; false positives lead to echocardiography referrals.
- **Distinguishing MMVD stages** in a dog with a known murmur — imaging and clinical criteria do that.
- **Screening cats for HCM.** A normal value does not exclude early hypertrophy.
- **Monitoring chronic therapy** in stable patients, where clinical signs and imaging drive decisions.

## Monitoring treatment

Where NT-proBNP is used serially — for example during hospitalisation for congestive heart failure — the trend can support the assessment of decongestion alongside respiratory rate, effort and weight. It should not replace those measures; it is an adjunct that can catch a patient whose clinical signs are equivocal.

## Six traps

1. **Screening asymptomatic animals.** False positives, unnecessary referrals.
2. **Ignoring renal function.** The commonest cause of a misleading high value.
3. **Treating the number rather than the patient.** Diuresis decisions need clinical assessment.
4. **Applying canine thresholds to cats.** Different biology and limits.
5. **Excluding early HCM on a normal value.** Imaging remains the reference.
6. **Comparing platforms mid-case.** Trends across systems are uninterpretable.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** Cardiac biomarkers are the clearest case in this menu where the honest marketing claim is narrower than the obvious one — and narrower sells better to the clinicians who matter.

**Reasoning.** The obvious claim is "detect heart disease early", which invites exactly the asymptomatic screening that produces false positives and eroded trust. The narrower claim — "resolve the dyspnoeic patient" — is bought by emergency and internal medicine clinicians who face that decision weekly, and it is the use case where a same-day result genuinely beats sending blood away. There is a second, less obvious benefit: a clinician who has used the test successfully to rule out heart failure once will trust it the next time a result is ambiguous. Trust in diagnostics is built from correct negatives far more than from impressive positives.
