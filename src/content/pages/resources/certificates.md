# Certificates and Quality System: Scope Matters More Than the Logo

**Quick Answer** — A certificate is a statement about a management system within a defined scope. It is not a product approval, and it does not cover activities outside the scope statement. Buyers who check the scope, the site and the issuing body's accreditation learn more in two minutes than a page of logos conveys.

## What to check on any certificate

| Field | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Certificate number | Verifiable with the issuing body? |
| Issuing body and accreditation | Is the body accredited for this standard? |
| Standard and revision | ISO 13485:2016, or an older revision? |
| Scope statement | Which activities? Design? Manufacture? Distribution? |
| Sites covered | Is the manufacturing site for my product listed? |
| Validity dates | Current, and not expiring within the contract period? |

## Why scope is the decisive field

A certificate that covers "distribution of in vitro diagnostic devices" is materially different from one covering "design and manufacture of fluorescence immunochromatographic diagnostic kits". Both may be genuine, and both may be presented as evidence of manufacturing capability — only one supports the claim.

Similarly, a multi-site certificate may list some sites and not others. If the product is made at a site not listed, the certificate does not describe the process that produced it.

## Certification versus product approval

| Instrument | What it certifies | What it does not |
|---|---|---|
| ISO 13485 | Quality management system for medical devices, within scope | Product approval, or performance |
| ISO 9001 | Quality management system, general | Any device-specific requirement |
| CE marking | Conformity with applicable EU legislation for a device class | Quality system certification |
| National registration | Market authorisation for a product in that jurisdiction | Manufacturing quality |

These are complementary, not substitutable. A dossier usually needs several of them, and a buyer who asks for "your ISO" without specifying which of the four is meant will get an incomplete picture.

## Supplier qualification questions

1. Which site manufactures the product I am buying, and is it on the certificate?
2. Does the scope include design, or only manufacture and distribution?
3. Which revision of the standard is certified?
4. Who is the issuing body, and is it accredited?
5. What is the certificate's expiry date, and what is the surveillance cycle?
6. Which product registrations exist in my market?

## Six traps

1. **Accepting a logo as evidence.** The scope statement is the evidence.
2. **Assuming a certificate covers all sites.** It may list only some.
3. **Confusing ISO 9001 with ISO 13485.** The latter carries device-specific requirements.
4. **Treating CE marking as a quality certificate.** Different instrument, different meaning.
5. **Ignoring the certificate's expiry.** A lapsed certificate in a dossier is a finding.
6. **Not verifying the certificate number.** Verification is free and fast.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** Publishing certificate scopes — not just logos — is a trust signal that most competitors cannot copy, because most cannot show a scope that includes design and manufacture.

**Reasoning.** In this market, the distinction between a manufacturer and a trading company is the single most consequential thing a buyer needs to establish, and certificate scope answers it directly. A trading company can hold a distribution-scope certificate and present it identically to a manufacturing one at a glance; the scope statement is what separates them. A manufacturer that publishes its scope, with the site and the activities named, has effectively answered the qualification question before the first meeting — and that is worth more than any number of compliance logos in a footer.
