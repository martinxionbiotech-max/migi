## What a validation summary contains

1. **Objective** — which performance characteristic was evaluated.
2. **Design** — replicates, days, lots, or sample counts and their selection.
3. **Population** — species, age, clinical status, and how cases and controls were defined.
4. **Comparator** — the reference method and its own limitations.
5. **Results** — figures with confidence intervals, not a single point estimate.
6. **Conclusion and limitations** — including what the study does not cover.

A summary missing any of these is a claim wearing a study's clothing.

## Why independent validation matters, and what we say about it

Third-party validation is the strongest form of evidence available in this category, because it removes the incentive problem inherent in manufacturer-generated data. Buyers are right to ask for it.

We have not published independent studies. The practical consequences:

- **Registration:** many markets accept manufacturer data in the technical file; check your specific requirement.
- **Tenders:** some evaluation processes score independent evidence explicitly; where that is decisive, we are at a disadvantage and it is better to know now.
- **Clinical adoption:** clinicians sometimes prefer a platform with external literature. That preference is reasonable.

## What a buyer should ask next

1. Which of the four evidence types exists for this specific analyte?
2. Was the reference interval established on the platform being sold, with what n?
3. Is the clinical performance study population representative of my caseload?
4. What is not yet covered, and when is it expected?
5. Is anything available under NDA that is not public?

## The Author's Take

**Position.** Publishing a validation status table that includes "not published" rows is more persuasive than a page that only lists completed studies, because it demonstrates that evidence is tracked rather than assembled for the website.

**Reasoning.** In this category the buyer is often a quality or regulatory professional whose job is to find what is missing. A vendor who lists gaps has already answered the first question of the audit, and the conversation moves to planning instead of discovery. A vendor who lists only strengths signals that the buyer's job is to find the omission — and the buyer will. The uncomfortable table is the one that shortens the qualification process, which is the opposite of what instinct suggests.
