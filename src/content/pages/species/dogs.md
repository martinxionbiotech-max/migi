# Dogs: What to Test, at Which Stage, and What to Skip

**Quick Answer** — Dogs are tested for four reasons: to confirm an infection that requires isolation, to grade inflammation and follow its response, to answer a time-critical breeding or cardiac question, and to monitor a disease already diagnosed. Anything outside those four is usually a test whose result will not change what happens to the dog.

## Quick Answer

A test earns its place by changing a decision. If the result will not change treatment, isolation, discharge or breeding timing, it is a cost with a number attached.

## By life stage

**Puppies (0–12 months)** — the questions are infectious disease and immunity, not chronic disease. Parvovirus and distemper antigen when clinical signs appear; antibody testing is confounded by maternal antibody and by vaccination, which is why antigen is the diagnostic anchor.

**Adults (1–7 years)** — most testing here is driven by a clinical episode: vomiting, diarrhoea, pyrexia, weight loss, or a breeding decision. Inflammatory markers support triage and, more importantly, monitoring. Pancreatic lipase resolves the ambiguous pancreatitis case. Progesterone and relaxin, in that order, answer two different breeding questions.

**Seniors (7+ years)** — the questions become chronic: renal function, cardiac load, endocrine disease, and low-grade inflammation. Testing shifts from diagnosis to monitoring, which means the same markers on the same platform over time matter more than any single panel.

## The high-value tests, by decision

| Decision | Test |
|---|---|
| Isolate or not | Parvovirus antigen, distemper antigen |
| Treat intensively or send home | Inflammatory markers (CRP), pancreatic lipase |
| Breed today or in 48 hours | Progesterone series |
| Is she pregnant or pseudopregnant | Relaxin |
| Cardiac or respiratory cause of dyspnoea | NT-proBNP |
| Is the treatment working | Serial CRP, serial cPL |
| Is this hypothyroidism or illness | T4 **with** TSH, never T4 alone |

The last row is deliberately phrased as a pair. A single low T4 is the most common route to a lifelong prescription in a dog that never needed one.

## What to skip

- **Broad panels in asymptomatic young dogs.** Low yield, incidental findings.
- **Antibody testing to diagnose acute infection.** Vaccination confounds it.
- **A single cortisol to diagnose Cushing's.** Dynamic testing is required.
- **Screening echocardiography substitutes.** A biomarker does not exclude structural disease.

## Six traps

1. **Testing without a decision attached.** The most common waste.
2. **Diagnosing on one abnormal value.** Most markers require context.
3. **Ignoring the vaccination history.** Changes antibody interpretation completely.
4. **Switching platforms mid-monitoring.** Bias mimics clinical change.
5. **Using a canine marker in a cat (or vice versa).** Species-specific antibodies and intervals.
6. **Repeating the same test without a changed clinical picture.** A second number rarely resolves an ambiguous first.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** A species hub should be built around decisions, not around a menu list, and it should be willing to say "do not test" more often than vendors usually do.

**Reasoning.** Clinics do not wake up wanting a panel; they wake up with a puppy that has bloody diarrhoea or a bitch that must be bred this week. Organising a species page around those moments makes the relevant assay obvious and makes the irrelevant ones visibly irrelevant, which is a service to the buyer and a defence for the vendor. It also reduces the most common source of dissatisfaction in this category: a test was run, the number came back, nobody knew what to do with it, and the assay took the blame.
