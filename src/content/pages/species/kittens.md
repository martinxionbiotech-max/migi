# Kittens: Panleukopenia, Retrovirus Timing and the Six-Month Rule

**Quick Answer** — Kitten testing has one overriding rule: **a result before about six months of age is provisional for anything antibody-based**. Maternal antibody protects, confounds, and then decays — and acting on a pre-window result can produce a lifelong diagnosis in a healthy cat. Antigen testing for acute disease is unaffected by this and should be used freely when a kitten is sick.

## Quick Answer

Antigen when the kitten is ill. Antibody only after the maternal window — and repeat it.

## What matters, in order

| Situation | Test | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Vomiting, diarrhoea, fever, collapse | Panleukopenia antigen | Fast, changes isolation and care |
| Respiratory signs | FCV / FHV antigen | Outbreak management in catteries |
| Unknown retrovirus status | FeLV antigen, FIV antibody | Screening, with the retest rule |
| Retrovirus positive before ~6 months | Repeat after the window | Maternal antibody |
| Pre-adoption | Per destination requirements | Movement decision, not diagnosis |

## The six-month rule

Maternal FIV antibody can persist for weeks to months. A positive antibody result in a young kitten may reflect transfer, not infection. The standard approach is to retest after the maternal antibody window rather than to declare the kitten infected.

Why this matters more than it appears: a positive result in a kitten can lead to rehoming refusal, relinquishment, or euthanasia. Those decisions are irreversible, and they are being made on a test that was never designed to answer the question at that age.

## Panleukopenia: the emergency

Feline panleukopenia is a parvovirus, and the clinical urgency matches canine parvovirus: rapid onset, high mortality in unprotected kittens, and a same-day answer that changes everything about isolation and supportive care.

Two technical points that belong in every training session:

1. **Cross-reactivity.** Canine parvovirus assays often detect feline panleukopenia virus. That is useful in an emergency, but it must be stated rather than assumed.
2. **Vaccine shedding.** Modified-live vaccines can produce transient positive faecal antigen results. A recently vaccinated kitten with mild signs may be antigen positive because of the vaccine.

## Reading results in a kitten

| Result | Interpretation in a kitten |
|---|---|
| Panleukopenia antigen positive, unvaccinated, ill | Treat as FPV; isolate |
| Panleukopenia antigen positive, recently vaccinated, mild signs | Vaccine shedding possible; correlate |
| FeLV antigen positive | Confirm; some kittens clear |
| FIV antibody positive, under 6 months | Provisional; retest after window |
| Any antibody positive, healthy kitten | Do not act on it before the retest interval |

## Six traps

1. **Acting on a pre-six-month FIV result.** The most damaging error in kitten testing.
2. **Declaring a FeLV-positive kitten permanent without confirmation.** Some clear.
3. **Assuming a canine CPV test cannot detect FPV.** It often can.
4. **Forgetting vaccine shedding.** A positive antigen in a recently vaccinated kitten is not automatically infection.
5. **Skipping isolation while waiting for confirmation.** In panleukopenia, hours matter.
6. **Testing healthy kittens for immunity instead of vaccinating on schedule.** The test does not protect.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** The kitten page should be written primarily to prevent premature decisions, because that is where the harm is concentrated.

**Reasoning.** Everything else on this site is about choosing the right test. In kittens, the dominant risk is not the wrong test — it is the right test at the wrong age, followed by an irreversible decision. A vendor that publishes the six-month rule, the confirmation pathway and the vaccine-shedding caveat is doing the one thing that this segment actually needs, and it is also protecting its own reputation: a cat euthanised on a maternal-antibody result is a story that circulates in the cat community for years.
