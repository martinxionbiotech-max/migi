# Cats: A Different Biology, a Different Testing Strategy

**Quick Answer** — Feline testing differs from canine testing in three ways that matter clinically: the acute-phase response is carried by SAA rather than CRP, retrovirus screening has specific rules about age and confirmation, and the thyroid–kidney interaction means two systems must be read together. A feline protocol built by adapting a canine one will be wrong in all three places.

## Quick Answer

Same menu, different interpretation. The species determines which marker, which window, and which confounder.

## Three species-specific differences

**1. Acute-phase response.** SAA is the dominant feline acute-phase protein; CRP rises modestly. Using CRP as the feline inflammation marker understates the response — and cats are the species where the clinician is most often working with thin examination findings.

**2. Retrovirus rules.** FeLV antigen and FIV antibody are the screening tests. Both have specific rules:

| Situation | Rule |
|---|---|
| Cat of unknown status | Screen once; retest if exposure risk continues |
| Kitten under ~6 months | Retest after the maternal antibody window before declaring positive |
| Low-risk cat, positive result | Confirm before a lifelong diagnosis is communicated |
| Vaccinated against FIV (where applicable) | Antibody cannot distinguish vaccination from infection |

**3. Thyroid and kidney.** Hyperthyroidism raises filtration and can mask reduced renal function. Treating the thyroid can unmask azotaemia, so T4 and renal parameters are measured together, before and after treatment — a rule that exists only in cats.

## The feline testing matrix

| Clinical picture | First-line tests |
|---|---|
| Acute upper respiratory signs | FCV and FHV antigen from oropharyngeal/conjunctival swabs |
| Effusion, fever of unknown origin, young cat | FCoV **on effusion**, with effusion protein and A:G ratio — not serum antibody |
| Vomiting, weight loss, jaundice | fPL plus SAA; assess liver and gut as well |
| Weight loss, polyphagia, palpable thyroid | T4 with renal parameters |
| Anorexia, non-specific illness | SAA, with renal and thyroid assessment |
| Chronic weight loss, anaemia, recurrent infection | FeLV antigen + FIV antibody together |

## What feline testing punishes

- **Assuming dogs' markers transfer.** CRP is a weak feline signal.
- **Diagnosing FIP from an antibody titre.** Exposure is near-universal in multi-cat environments; disease is rare.
- **Declaring a kitten FIV-positive.** Maternal antibody is expected.
- **Treating hyperthyroidism without checking renal function.** Azotaemia gets unmasked.
- **Excluding hyperthyroidism on one normal T4.** Early disease sits inside the interval.

## Handling and stress

Cats tolerate handling poorly, and sample quality suffers: small volumes, difficult venepuncture, delayed separation. For analytes sensitive to handling, the practical answer is to control the pre-analytical chain — minimise handling, separate promptly, and record the delay.

## Six traps

1. **Canine markers in cats.** Understates inflammation.
2. **Antibody for FIP diagnosis.** The most consequential feline interpretation error.
3. **One normal T4 as a rule-out.** Repeat testing is the standard.
4. **Unconfirmed retrovirus positives.** Confirm before lifelong advice.
5. **Ignoring renal function when treating thyroid disease.** Classic oversight.
6. **Poor sample handling in a difficult venepuncture.** The result reflects the sample, not the cat.

**Position.** Feline content should be written by someone who works with cats, because the failure modes here are feline-specific and a canine page with "cat" substituted in will actively mislead.

**Reasoning.** The three differences above are not trivia — each one changes a diagnosis.
