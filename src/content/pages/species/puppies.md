# Puppies: Maternal Antibody, Vaccination Windows and What a Test Actually Tells You

**Quick Answer** — Puppy testing is dominated by one biological fact: maternal antibody. It protects the puppy, it blocks vaccination, it confounds antibody tests, and it decays on a schedule that varies between individuals. That is why a puppy's test result is always a snapshot with an expiry date, and why the highest-value intervention in this age group is vaccination and exposure control, not testing.

## Quick Answer

Test the sick puppy for antigen. Do not test the healthy puppy for immunity and call it protection.

## The two questions in the first year

| Question | Useful test | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Is this puppy infected now? | Parvovirus / distemper antigen | Window, sample quality, antigen excess |
| Is this puppy protected? | Antibody (where validated) | Snapshot; protective thresholds vary by pathogen |
| When should vaccines be given? | None — schedule-based | Maternal antibody decay is individual |
| Is this puppy's diarrhoea infectious? | Antigen, with coronavirus considered | Co-infection changes the picture |

## The maternal antibody problem, stated plainly

Maternal antibody is transferred in colostrum and declines over weeks to months. While it is present it neutralises both wild virus and vaccine. The result is the **susceptibility gap**: a period when the puppy has lost maternal protection but has not yet responded to vaccination.

No test closes that gap. The practical responses are:

1. **Repeat vaccination** through the risk period, per the current guideline schedule.
2. **Control exposure** — isolation from unknown-status animals, hygiene, cohort management.
3. **Test when signs appear**, not to predict protection.

## When puppy testing changes management

- **Haemorrhagic diarrhoea** — parvovirus antigen; isolation and supportive care depend on it.
- **Neurological or respiratory signs with fever** — distemper antigen, acknowledging that antigen may be undetectable at the neurological stage.
- **Outbreak in a litter or shelter intake** — antigen testing with cohorting; see the shelter page for the timeline.
- **Pre-transfer testing** where the destination requires it.

## Reading a negative result

Four mechanisms produce a false negative in this age group:

1. **Timing** — too early, or after shedding has declined.
2. **Sample** — poor swab technique, insufficient material.
3. **Antigen excess** — very high viral loads read low; dilute to confirm.
4. **Interference** — haemolysis or contamination.

In a puppy with compatible signs, a negative result means "repeat", not "discharge".

## Six traps

1. **Using antibody to predict protection.** A snapshot, not a forecast.
2. **Treating a vaccinated puppy as immune.** Maternal antibody blocks vaccine take.
3. **Excluding parvovirus on one negative.** Repeat.
4. **Ignoring antigen excess in severe cases.** Dilute.
5. **Testing healthy puppies instead of scheduling vaccines.** The test does not prevent disease.
6. **Comparing results across platforms.** Cut-offs differ.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** The most valuable puppy-page content is the honest statement that no test substitutes for vaccination timing — and that messaging protects the vendor from selling a false promise.

**Reasoning.** Puppy owners frequently ask for a test to "check immunity", and clinics are tempted to offer one because it produces a billable result. But an antibody snapshot taken during the susceptibility gap can be reassuring and wrong, and a negative result can prompt unnecessary revaccination or, worse, a false sense of security. A vendor that says "test for infection, vaccinate for protection" keeps the platform in the role where it is reliable and avoids the scenario where a puppy dies of parvovirus two weeks after a "protective" result — a scenario in which the assay, unfairly, takes the blame.
