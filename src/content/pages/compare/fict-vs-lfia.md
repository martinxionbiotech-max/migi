# FICT vs Lateral Flow (Colloidal Gold)

**Quick Answer** — The difference is not sensitivity, it is **what the result is**. A gold strip produces a judgement; a fluorescence cartridge produces a measurement. That matters when you need to trend a patient, audit a decision, or compare across operators — and it does not matter when the question is binary and the answer will be acted on immediately in a setting with no records.

## The comparison that actually matters

| Dimension | Colloidal gold (LFIA) | Fluorescence (FICT) |
|---|---|---|
| Output | Line present / absent, or band intensity | Concentration with an interval and a unit |
| Dynamic range | Narrow — saturates visually | Wide — gradations preserved |
| Reading | Human eye, lighting dependent | Instrument |
| Operator variation | Interpretation varies | Interpretation removed; handling remains |
| Record | Written by hand, if at all | Stored, exportable, auditable |
| Trending | Only across a visible threshold | Against assay imprecision |
| Equipment | None | Analyzer required |
| Unit cost | Lower | Higher per test, lower per useful data point in monitoring |
| Failure modes | Faint line ambiguity, delayed reading | Calibration, QC, reagent handling |

## Where a strip is the right answer

It is easy to write a comparison that flatters the instrument. The honest version:

- **Binary triage where the result is acted on immediately** and no trend is needed.
- **Settings without power, space or trained staff** for an instrument.
- **Very low test volume**, where maintaining calibration and QC costs more than the information is worth.
- **Field and shelter screening** where the goal is sorting animals into isolate / do not isolate.

An instrument that is not maintained produces worse information than a strip that is read carefully.

## Where fluorescence earns its cost

1. **Monitoring.** A strip can tell you a value crossed a threshold; it cannot tell you by how much, or whether the change exceeds noise.
2. **Audit and liability.** An instrument-read result with a timestamp and an operator ID is defensible in a way a hand-written impression is not.
3. **Multi-operator consistency.** In a busy clinic, interpretation differences between staff are real and largely invisible without an instrument.
4. **Data capture.** Numeric results feed records, reports and research; visual impressions do not.

## The trap in the comparison

The common misreading is "quantitative equals more sensitive". Analytical sensitivity and quantitative output are different properties: an assay can be quantitative and less sensitive than a good strip, or highly sensitive and qualitative.

## Where both are appropriate

Many clinics end up running both: strips for immediate triage where no record is needed, the instrument for anything that will be trended, billed as a data point, or audited. That is a rational portfolio, not a compromise.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** The comparison should be framed as "what kind of result do you need", not "which technology is better" — and a vendor that concedes the strip's legitimate uses will be believed on the instrument's advantages.

**Reasoning.** Buyers in this category have usually used strips, often for years, and they know their limitations from experience. A comparison that presents fluorescence as universally superior contradicts that experience, and the buyer resolves the contradiction by discounting the vendor. A comparison that says "a strip is fine for X and fails at Y" reads as technical confidence rather than sales pressure — and it also sets up the strongest argument for the instrument, which is not sensitivity but the ability to prove what happened.
