# FICT vs PCR: Protein or Genome

**Quick Answer** — An antigen test detects a protein from the organism; PCR detects its genetic material. That single difference explains almost every practical distinction: PCR can be positive earlier and can remain positive after the organism is no longer viable, PCR identifies strain or sequence, and PCR costs more and takes hours. Antigen testing is faster and cheaper; PCR is more sensitive and more specific in ways that matter in defined situations.

## The comparison

| Dimension | Antigen (FICT) | PCR |
|---|---|---|
| Target | Viral/bacterial protein | Nucleic acid sequence |
| Window | During antigen presence — narrower | Earlier and often longer |
| Speed | Minutes | Hours to days |
| Sample handling | Field-friendly | Requires specific transport and preservation |
| Typing/sequencing | No | Yes |
| Contamination risk | Low | Real — amplicon and cross-sample |
| Cost per test | Lower | Higher |
| Use in clinic | Triage and monitoring | Confirmation, typing, discordant cases |

## The window difference, concretely

A negative antigen test in an animal that is early in infection is expected, not contradictory. A positive PCR in an animal that has recovered can reflect residual nucleic acid rather than active disease. Both facts are routinely ignored, and both produce confident wrong conclusions:

- **"The antigen test was negative, so it is not parvovirus."** Possibly tested before detectable antigen.
- **"The PCR is still positive, so treatment failed."** Possibly residual genome after clinical resolution.

The window, not the assay, decides which statement is defensible.

## Where PCR is irreplaceable

1. **Strain identification** — for outbreak investigation, vaccine match, and virulence markers.
2. **Resolving a discordant antigen result** in a strongly suspected case.
3. **Agents without a reliable antigen test** in the local menu.
4. **Quantitative viral load** where that changes management.
5. **Confirming a diagnosis when the antigen window has passed.**

## Where antigen testing is sufficient

- **Acute triage**: is this puppy shedding parvovirus now, and does it need isolation today?
- **Outbreak decisions** where same-minute answers determine cohorting.
- **Monitoring response** where the antigen level tracks clinical state.
- **Resource-limited settings** where PCR is unavailable or unaffordable.

## Using them together

A defensible sequence in most clinics:

1. **Antigen first** when speed changes action.
2. **PCR for discordance** — negative antigen with strong clinical suspicion, or positive antigen with an atypical picture.
3. **PCR for typing** when the result will influence a population-level decision (outbreak, breeding programme, regulatory report).

## The specificity claim, examined

PCR is often described as more specific. That is true analytically, but clinic-level specificity also depends on contamination control, sample handling and laboratory quality. An antigen assay run carefully can be more reliable than a PCR run badly — which is why method claims should be qualified by the setting, not asserted absolutely.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** The comparison should be framed around windows and decisions rather than around analytical superiority, because the superiority claim is the one that gets tested first and fails first.

**Reasoning.** Every laboratory-trained buyer knows PCR is more sensitive; a vendor who claims otherwise loses credibility immediately. What buyers often do not have is a working rule for when the cheaper, faster test is sufficient — and that rule is the vendor's real contribution. Saying "antigen for triage, PCR for discordance and typing" gives the clinician a defensible pathway, positions PCR as a complement rather than a competitor, and makes the point-of-care assay the default first step instead of the compromise option.
