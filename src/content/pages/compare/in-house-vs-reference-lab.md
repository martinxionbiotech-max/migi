# In-House vs Reference Laboratory: The Real Cost Is the Wait

**Quick Answer** — Comparing cartridge price with laboratory price is the wrong comparison, and it is the one most clinics make. The correct comparison is fully loaded cost per test — cartridge, controls, staff time, instrument — against fully loaded send-out cost including the clinical consequence of a delay. Once the delay is priced, the split is usually obvious: in-clinic for decisions, send-out for everything else.

## The comparison, done properly

| Cost component | In-house | Send-out |
|---|---|---|
| Per-test reagent | Higher | Lower |
| Controls and QC | Clinic's time and materials | Included in laboratory operation |
| Staff time | Collection + run + record | Collection + packaging + courier |
| Transport, packaging | None | Real, and often forgotten |
| Delay to result | Minutes | Hours to days |
| Consequence of delay | Minimal | Hospitalisation, second visit, empirical treatment |
| Result provenance | Your own record | Accredited laboratory report |
| Menu breadth | Limited | Broad |

## Pricing the delay

The delay is not an abstraction. In a suspect parvovirus case it is a day of ward space and cohort exposure. In a breeding management case it is a cycle. In a dyspnoeic cat it is diuretics started blind. In post-operative monitoring it is a complication identified a day later.

The honest version of this calculation includes the tests that **would not** have changed anything — because those are the ones that make in-house capacity expensive if the menu is bought indiscriminately.

## Where send-out is the right answer

1. **Rare analytes** where the cartridge would expire before use.
2. **Batch screening** — a breeding programme, a study cohort, a shelter intake panel.
3. **Confirmatory testing** where a second method is the point.
4. **Traceability-sensitive work** — regulatory, legal, research endpoints.
5. **Anything that will not be acted on today.**

## Where in-house wins

1. **Isolation decisions** — a positive or negative result changes where the animal goes within the hour.
2. **Treatment decisions in unstable patients** — fluids, antibiotics, diuretics.
3. **Time-critical breeding decisions.**
4. **Clients who will not return** for the result.
5. **Outbreak settings** where transmission continues while a batch is pending.

## The hybrid rule

The useful decision rule is per-test, not per-clinic:

| Test characteristic | Where it belongs |
|---|---|
| Changes action within hours | In-house |
| Changes action within a day, animal stable | Either — cost decides |
| Screens asymptomatic animals | Send-out, or not at all |
| Confirms a diagnosis already suspected | Send-out unless timing matters |
| Uncommon, low volume | Send-out |

## The comparison nobody publishes

Most vendor comparisons assume every in-house test changes a decision and ignore the expired-cartridge problem. An honest page states the opposite: in-house capacity is efficient when the menu is narrow and used frequently, and expensive when it is broad and used occasionally.

## Six traps

1. **Comparing reagent price with laboratory price.** Ignores the delay and the overheads.
2. **Buying a broad menu.** Expiry is a real cost line.
3. **Forgetting staff time.** Especially in understaffed clinics.
4. **Ignoring the tests that did not change anything.** They are part of the economics.
5. **Sending out tests that are decided today.** The delay is the whole point.
6. **Comparing results across methods later.** Bias, not equivalence.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** The honest commercial position is "in-house for decisions, send-out for everything else" — and a vendor that says this sells fewer menus and keeps more accounts.

**Reasoning.** Clinics that buy a broad in-house menu to "save money" typically discover expiry and underuse within a year, and the resulting disappointment attaches to the platform rather than to the purchasing decision. A vendor that helps the clinic size the menu to its decision load ends up with a customer who uses the instrument daily, renews consumables predictably, and recommends it to peers — because it did what it was bought for. The narrower sale is the durable one, and in a category where trust is the scarce resource, that is the better trade.
