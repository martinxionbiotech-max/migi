# FICT vs ELISA: Single Test Against Batch

**Quick Answer** — ELISA and point-of-care fluorescence are both immunoassays; the difference is **where the work happens and when the answer arrives**. ELISA buys precision and cost per test at volume, at the price of hours to days of latency. A point-of-care cartridge buys same-visit latency, at a higher cost per test and with performance dependent on clinic handling. The decision is about the decision the result will serve.

## The comparison

| Dimension | ELISA (laboratory) | FICT (point-of-care) |
|---|---|---|
| Throughput model | Batch — plates of samples | Single sample, on demand |
| Turnaround | Hours to next day | Minutes |
| Cost per test at volume | Lower | Higher |
| Cost per test at low volume | High (controls, plates, labour) | Comparable or lower |
| Precision | High, with established controls | Good, dependent on handling |
| Operator skill | Trained technician | Clinic staff |
| Result comparability | Method-specific | Method-specific |
| Best use | Screening, research endpoints, panels | Same-day clinical decisions |

## Where ELISA still wins

1. **Batch screening.** Population studies, breeding programmes, research cohorts — economies of scale are real.
2. **Research endpoints** requiring traceable values and documented plate-level QC.
3. **Panels** where many analytes are measured from one sample.
4. **Low urgency**: a result that will be reviewed tomorrow does not need to be produced today.

## Where point-of-care wins

1. **Acute presentations** where treatment, isolation or discharge depends on the answer.
2. **Single samples** where the fixed costs of a plate cannot be amortised.
3. **Clients who will not return** for a follow-up visit to receive a result.
4. **Outbreak settings** where same-day isolation decisions prevent transmission.

## The precision question, asked properly

The useful comparison is not "POCT versus ELISA" but **the CV of the specific assay at the relevant concentration**. A well-run ELISA can have CVs in the low single digits; a poorly handled cartridge can be worse than the insert states. Conversely, a well-run cartridge can outperform an ELISA that is batched irregularly with infrequent controls.

## The bias question

Different immunoassays produce different absolute values for the same analyte. A clinic switching from a laboratory ELISA to a point-of-care assay will see a systematic shift, and any reference interval or decision limit carried across will be wrong.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** The honest framing is that ELISA remains the better instrument for batch work and POCT is the better instrument for decisions — and a vendor that says so converts more informed buyers than one that claims outright superiority.

**Reasoning.** Laboratory-trained buyers know that batch assays are efficient and precise; a claim of general superiority invites scepticism and a test drive that ends in a comparison table the vendor did not control. The narrower claim — "we are not trying to replace your plate reader, we are trying to replace the two-day wait on the cases that cannot wait" — is credible, fits the buyer's existing workflow, and positions the platform as an addition rather than a threat. That is a far easier procurement conversation, especially where the laboratory director is protective of existing validated methods.
