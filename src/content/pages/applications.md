# Applications: Testing Guidance by Setting

The same assay answers a different question depending on where it is run. These pages are written for the decisions each setting actually makes, including the ones where in-house testing is not the right answer.

## The six settings

| Setting | The decision it is built around |
|---|---|
| [Veterinary clinic](/applications/veterinary-clinic/) | Capacity, menu fit and workflow — sized to peak day, not average |
| [Reference laboratory](/applications/reference-laboratory/) | Where POCT fits (STAT, overflow, after-hours, backup) and where it does not |
| [Breeding kennel](/applications/breeding-kennel/) | Missed-cycle economics and a schedule kennel staff can execute |
| [Animal shelter](/applications/animal-shelter/) | Outbreak timeline, cohorting, and budget triage |
| [Research laboratory](/applications/research-laboratory/) | Screening and stratification versus traceable endpoints; lot bridging |
| [Mobile practice](/applications/mobile-vet/) | Temperature, power, bench space and a QC rhythm for vehicles |

## The common error

Buying for the catalogue rather than for the decision load. A clinic that acquires a broad menu and runs three assays has paid for flexibility it does not use, and the expiring reagents become the memory of the purchase.

The rule that prevents most of it: **test to decide, not to know.** If a result will not change treatment, isolation, discharge, or breeding timing today, it can go to a laboratory — or not be ordered.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** Setting-based pages are the most commercially useful content on a diagnostic site, because buyers search for their situation, not for an analyte.

**Reasoning.** A clinic director does not search "quantitative fluorescence immunoassay"; they search for whether in-house testing pays back, or how to handle a parvo outbreak in a shelter. Content organised by setting meets that query directly, and it also does something a product page cannot: it disagrees with the reader's plan when the plan is wrong. Telling a low-volume clinic that they do not need an analyzer costs one sale and buys a reputation, and in this market reputation is the mechanism by which the second sale happens.
