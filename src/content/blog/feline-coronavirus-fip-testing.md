Feline coronavirus (FCoV) is one of the most common — and most misunderstood — infections in cats. Most infected cats show no signs, but in a small minority, the virus mutates into the devastating disease feline infectious peritonitis (FIP). Understanding the distinction between coronavirus infection and FIP is essential for interpreting test results correctly.

## Feline Coronavirus vs. FIP

### Feline Coronavirus (FCoV)
- Extremely common, especially in multi-cat environments.
- Usually causes mild, transient diarrhoea or no signs at all.
- Spread via faeces and shared litter boxes.

### Feline Infectious Peritonitis (FIP)
- Occurs when FCoV **mutates** within an individual cat, enabling it to infect macrophages and spread systemically.
- Historically fatal; new antiviral treatments have improved outcomes.
- Two forms: **effusive ("wet")** — fluid accumulation in the abdomen or chest; and **non-effusive ("dry")** — granulomatous lesions in organs.

**The critical point:** FCoV infection is common; FIP is rare. A positive coronavirus test does **not** mean a cat has FIP.

## How Coronavirus Testing Works

**FCoV antigen testing** detects the presence of coronavirus in faecal or other samples. It confirms that a cat is infected with — or shedding — coronavirus.

## Why a Positive Test Isn't FIP

This is the most important interpretive point in feline coronavirus testing:

- A positive FCoV antigen result means **coronavirus infection**, which is very common and usually benign.
- FIP is a **clinical diagnosis** — it requires the combination of compatible clinical signs, physical findings (such as effusion), and supporting laboratory data.

No single blood or antigen test can definitively diagnose FIP on its own. Over-interpreting a positive coronavirus test leads to unnecessary anxiety and, historically, to unnecessary euthanasia.

## How FIP Is Diagnosed

FIP diagnosis is multimodal and includes:

1. **Clinical signs** — persistent fever, weight loss, abdominal or chest effusion, ocular or neurological signs.
2. **Effusion analysis** — the fluid in "wet" FIP has characteristic features (high protein, specific cell patterns).
3. **Blood markers** — elevated globulins, low albumin:globulin ratio, elevated acute-phase proteins.
4. **Antibody titres** — high coronavirus antibody titres support (but do not confirm) FIP.
5. **Molecular tests** — PCR on effusion or tissue can detect the mutated virus.

## The Role of Antigen Testing in Practice

FCoV antigen testing is valuable for:

- **Screening multi-cat households and shelters** — identifying shedders to inform hygiene protocols.
- **Investigating persistent diarrhoea** — coronavirus as a possible cause.
- **Providing context** — knowing coronavirus status when FIP is suspected.

It is a **screening and epidemiological tool**, not a standalone FIP diagnostic.

## When Should You Test?

- **Persistent or recurrent diarrhoea** — consider FCoV as a cause.
- **Suspected FIP** — coronavirus status provides context for the diagnostic workup.
- **Multi-cat facility management** — identify and manage shedding.

## Conclusion

Feline coronavirus testing is valuable — but only when interpreted correctly. A positive result means coronavirus infection, not FIP. By understanding this distinction and using antigen testing as part of a broader diagnostic picture, clinicians can avoid misdiagnosis and provide cats with accurate, compassionate care.
