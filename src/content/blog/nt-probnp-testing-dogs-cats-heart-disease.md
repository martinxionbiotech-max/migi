# NT-proBNP Testing in Dogs and Cats: Detecting Heart Disease Early

> **TL;DR** — NT-proBNP is released when heart muscle stretches under load. In dogs, >900 pmol/L raises suspicion and >1800 pmol/L strongly suggests significant heart disease; in cats, >270 pmol/L supports cardiac disease. Its highest-value use is triaging the dyspneic patient — is it the heart or the lungs? Quantitative testing returns a number in 10–20 minutes, letting you answer that question in the exam room instead of the next day.

---

## In Plain Terms

Picture a balloon. The harder you inflate it, the tighter the rubber stretches — and the more the balloon complains. The heart works the same way: under pressure or volume overload, its muscle stretches and releases NT-proBNP as a chemical "I'm working too hard" signal. A low NT-proBNP means the balloon is comfortably inflated. A high one means it's straining.

---

## Why Heart Disease Slips Through

Here's a scene every clinician knows. A 12-year-old cat comes in breathing fast. The owner thinks it's a hairball or "just the heat." A 10-year-old Cavalier has been coughing for a month, treated twice for "kennel cough." A middle-aged Doberman collapses on a walk.

Heart disease in dogs and cats is quiet. Valvular disease and cardiomyopathy develop over months to years with few outward signs — until the day the patient decompensates and presents in respiratory distress. That first obvious presentation is often already an emergency.

The diagnostic challenge is that **clinical signs of cardiac disease look exactly like signs of respiratory disease.** A coughing dog could have mitral valve disease or collapsing trachea. A tachypneic cat could have congestive heart failure or feline asthma. Getting this wrong is expensive and dangerous — treating a cardiac patient with diuretics on a guess is bad medicine, and missing cardiac disease is worse.

This is exactly the gap NT-proBNP fills.

---

## What Is NT-proBNP?

B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) is a hormone the heart releases when its muscle stretches — under pressure overload, volume overload, or wall stress. The hormone is cleaved into two pieces: the active BNP and an inactive N-terminal fragment called **NT-proBNP**. The inactive fragment is more stable in blood, so it's the one we measure.

> The physiology is simple: **stressed heart muscle → more NT-proBNP in the bloodstream.** A low level means the heart is not under significant load; a high level means it is. The magnitude roughly tracks the severity of that load.

NT-proBNP is cleared by the kidneys, which matters — azotemic cats can have falsely elevated values. Interpret any result alongside renal status, especially in older cats where CKD and cardiac disease often coexist.

## Reference Ranges: The Numbers to Know

| Species | Normal | Grey Zone | Strongly Suggestive |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dog** | <900 pmol/L | 900–1800 pmol/L | >1800 pmol/L |
| **Cat** | <100 pmol/L | 100–270 pmol/L | >270 pmol/L |

A couple of things worth noting about these numbers:

- **The dog cutoff of ~900 pmol/L** detects congestive heart failure with roughly 85% sensitivity and 81% specificity — useful, but not perfect, which is why the grey zone exists.
- **The cat cutoff of ~270 pmol/L** is tuned to distinguish cardiac from respiratory causes of dyspnea, with sensitivity in the 85–92% range.
- **Grey-zone results aren't negatives.** A dog at 1,200 pmol/L isn't "normal." It means re-test, add imaging, or watch the trend.

## The Killer App: Triaging the Dyspneic Patient

If you take one thing from this article, make it this: **NT-proBNP's highest-value use is in the patient that can't tell you where it hurts.**

### The dyspneic cat

A cat in respiratory distress is one of the most fragile patients in practice — and one of the hardest to work up. Stress can kill these patients, so the less handling the better. The central question is: is this congestive heart failure or primary respiratory disease (asthma, pleural effusion from other causes, pneumonia)?

- NT-proBNP **<100 pmol/L** → cardiac disease unlikely; pursue respiratory causes.
- NT-proBNP **>270 pmol/L** → cardiac disease likely; stabilize and get an echo.

That single number, available in 10–20 minutes from a blood draw, changes the entire management plan — and it does so without the stress of a full diagnostic workup on a fragile patient.

### The coughing dog

Older small-breed dogs cough for two very different reasons: left-sided heart enlargement compressing the airway, or primary airway disease (collapsing trachea, chronic bronchitis). A dog with a normal NT-proBNP and a cough is more likely an airway case; a dog with an elevated NT-proBNP needs a cardiac workup before you blame the trachea.

### Screening at-risk breeds

Breeds with high rates of heart disease — Cavalier King Charles Spaniels, Dobermans, Boxers, Maine Coons, Ragdolls — benefit from NT-proBNP as part of routine screening. A rising value over a year, even within the "normal" range, can be an early warning to pursue an echo before clinical signs appear.

## NT-proBNP vs. Echocardiography: A Division of Labor

A common mistake is treating NT-proBNP and echo as competitors. They answer different questions:

| NT-proBNP | Echocardiography |
|---|---|
| Is the heart under stress? | Why is the heart under stress? |
| Screen + triage + monitor trend | Characterize + stage + guide therapy |
| 10–20 min, minimal handling | Requires equipment + often sedation |
| Blood test | Imaging |

The workflow that works: **NT-proBNP screens, echo confirms and characterizes, NT-proBNP then monitors** the response to therapy over months. Serial NT-proBNP is also a useful prognostic tool — a value that keeps climbing despite treatment is a red flag.

## Sample Handling and What Can Skew the Result

NT-proBNP is measured from serum or plasma, and a few pre-analytical points deserve attention:

- **Renal status is the big one.** NT-proBNP is cleared by the kidneys, so azotemic patients — especially cats with CKD — can have falsely elevated values. Always interpret NT-proBNP alongside creatinine and SDMA.
- **Hemolysis.** Hemolyzed samples can interfere with the assay; recollect visibly pink samples.
- **Age.** NT-proBNP drifts upward with age in cats, so a borderline value in an elderly cat deserves extra caution.
- **Stability.** Follow the kit's storage guidance — NT-proBNP is reasonably stable, but don't leave samples sitting out longer than the insert allows.

The practical takeaway: a high NT-proBNP in a patient with normal kidneys is a strong cardiac signal. A high NT-proBNP in a cat with CKD needs imaging to sort out.

## How the Test Works

NT-proBNP is measured from serum or plasma on a quantitative immunofluorescence analyzer. The process:

1. Draw blood (serum or plasma; check the specific kit for whole-blood compatibility).
2. Load the sample into the NT-proBNP cartridge.
3. The analyzer incubates the reaction and quantifies the fluorescent signal against a calibration curve.
4. Read the result in pmol/L within roughly 10–20 minutes.

Because the result is quantitative, you can store it, compare it to the reference range, and — most importantly — compare it to the patient's *previous* values to see whether the heart is getting better or worse.

## Interpreting a Grey-Zone Result

The grey zone (900–1800 pmol/L in dogs; 100–270 pmol/L in cats) is where most clinical uncertainty lives. Here's how to handle it:

1. **Check renal status.** Azotemia falsely elevates NT-proBNP, especially in cats. A high value with concurrent CKD is ambiguous — lean on imaging.
2. **Repeat in 1–3 months.** The trend is more informative than a single value. A stable grey-zone value in a non-clinical patient is reassuring; a climbing one is not.
3. **Add imaging.** When NT-proBNP sits in the grey zone and clinical signs are present, that's a signal to get the echo sooner rather than later.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. NT-proBNP is a **cardiac stress marker** — high values mean the heart is working under load, not necessarily that failure is present.
2. Its highest-value use is **triaging the dyspneic or coughing patient** into cardiac vs. respiratory pathways.
3. **Dog: >900 pmol/L suspicious, >1800 strongly suggestive. Cat: >270 pmol/L supports cardiac disease.**
4. NT-proBNP **screens and monitors; echo characterizes.** They're partners, not rivals.
5. Quantitative results let you **track the trend**, which is where the real clinical signal lives.
