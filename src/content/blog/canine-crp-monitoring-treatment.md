> **TL;DR** — C-reactive protein is the dog's major acute-phase protein, and its kinetics are what make it useful: it rises within **4–24 hours** of an inflammatory trigger, peaks at **24–48 hours**, and — with a short half-life — falls just as fast once the inflammation is controlled. Normal is low (healthy dogs typically under ~**10 mg/L**), but severe inflammation can push it into the **hundreds of mg/L**. That is why **serial** CRP, not a single value, is the tool: a falling curve after starting antibiotics or surgery means the treatment is working; a rising or stubbornly high curve means it is not. The one thing CRP will never tell you is *what* is causing the inflammation — it grades and tracks, it does not diagnose.

---

## In Plain Terms

Think of CRP as the fire alarm and the thermometer in one. When tissue is inflamed, the liver cranks out CRP within hours; when the inflammation is put out, CRP drains away almost as fast. That speed is the whole point — it lets you watch a treatment working in near real time, instead of waiting days to see whether the patient "looks better."

---

## The Kinetics That Make It Useful

| Phase | Timing | CRP behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | 4–24 h after trigger | Begins to rise |
| Peak | 24–48 h | Maximum |
| Resolution | Hours after control | Falls rapidly |

Compare this to a white blood cell count, which is slow and blunt. CRP is fast and sensitive — it moves before the patient visibly improves or deteriorates, which is why it is the preferred marker for *tracking* rather than diagnosing.

---

## Reading the Number

- **Normal** — healthy dogs are low, typically below ~**10 mg/L** (lab-dependent).
- **Mild-moderate** — tens of mg/L, seen in localised or resolving inflammation.
- **Severe** — hundreds of mg/L, seen in sepsis, severe infection, or major inflammation.

The dynamic range is the feature: a value that can move from 10 to 500 and back again is a value that can tell you where you are on the curve. But the absolute number matters less than its direction.

---

## Serial Monitoring: The Real Use Case

The three situations where serial CRP earns its keep:

1. **Response to antibiotics.** Start treatment, then re-measure CRP at 24–48 hours. A falling value confirms the therapy is hitting the infection; a flat or rising value is an early signal to reconsider the drug, the dose, or the diagnosis — days before clinical signs would tell you the same thing.

2. **Post-surgical recovery.** CRP rises after surgery (normal, peaking 24–48 h), then should fall steadily. A CRP that stays high or rises again after day 2–3 raises suspicion of a complication — infection, dehiscence, or an abscess — before it is clinically obvious.

3. **Chronic disease management.** In immune-mediated or inflammatory disease, a falling CRP tracks remission and a rising one flags a flare, letting you adjust therapy objectively.

---

## The Honest Limitation

CRP has one clear weakness, and it is important to say it plainly: **CRP is not specific.** It tells you inflammation exists and how intense it is, but not whether the cause is infection, cancer, trauma, or immune-mediated disease. A dog with a broken leg and a dog with sepsis can both have a high CRP.

The consequence is that CRP is a **monitoring tool, not a diagnostic oracle.** It grades severity and tracks treatment response; it does not make the diagnosis. Used that way — as a fast scoreboard rather than a standalone verdict — it is one of the most useful tests in small-animal medicine.

## How the Test Runs

1. Draw blood and run a quantitative CRP on an immunofluorescence analyzer — a numeric mg/L result in minutes.

4. Act on the trend: falling = working, rising = reassess.

## FAQ

### What is CRP and why does it matter?

The dog's major acute-phase protein — it rises within **4–24 h** of inflammation and falls fast on resolution, making it a real-time inflammation scoreboard.

### How fast does CRP rise and fall?

Rises in **4–24 h**, peaks at **24–48 h**, falls within hours of control (short half-life). That speed is what makes serial CRP useful.

### What is a normal CRP?

Healthy dogs are typically under ~**10 mg/L**; severe inflammation can reach **hundreds of mg/L**.

### Why serial instead of single?

A **falling** CRP (re-check at 24–48 h) means treatment is working; a **rising** one means it is not. The trend, not the single number, drives decisions.

### What does high CRP mean after surgery?

A normal post-op rise peaking at **24–48 h**, then a steady fall. A CRP that stays high or rises after day **2–3** suggests a complication.

### Is CRP specific?

No — it grades severity (<10 to 100s of mg/L) and tracks response but does not diagnose the cause. A monitoring tool, not a diagnostic oracle.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Speed is the feature** — CRP rises in **4–24 h**, peaks at **24–48 h**, and falls within hours. It moves before the patient visibly changes.
2. **Track the trend, not the value** — a falling CRP after antibiotics/surgery means "working"; a rising one means "reassess." Re-check at 24–48 h and read the slope.
3. **Post-op CRP is a complication detector** — a value that stays high or climbs after day **2–3** flags infection/dehiscence before it is obvious.
4. **Know the range** — normal under ~**10 mg/L**, severe in the **hundreds**. The huge dynamic range is what makes the trend meaningful.
5. **CRP grades, it doesn't diagnose** — it tells you inflammation is present and how bad (from <10 to 100s of mg/L), but never *why*. Use it to monitor, not name the disease.
