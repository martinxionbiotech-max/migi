> **TL;DR** — Progesterone is the pregnancy-sustaining hormone in the bitch: after ovulation, the corpus luteum must keep it elevated for the full ~**63-day** gestation. That is why the number you cared about for timing (the breeding window) becomes a *survival* number once she is pregnant. The rule of thumb: progesterone should stay comfortably above ~**5 ng/mL** through gestation, and a fall below ~**2 ng/mL** is associated with pregnancy loss. The reason for **serial** testing is that the *trend* is the signal — a corpus luteum that is quietly failing shows a falling curve days before the pregnancy is lost, and that is the window when supplementation can still rescue the litter.

---

## In Plain Terms

The same progesterone you used to time the breeding has a second, quieter job: keeping the pregnancy alive. Once the bitch is pregnant, the corpus luteum (the structure left behind after ovulation) becomes the pregnancy's life-support system, pumping out progesterone to keep the uterus calm and the puppies attached. If that life support starts to fail, the first sign is not bleeding or illness — it is a number on a blood test, drifting downward.

---

## The Two Jobs of Progesterone

| Phase | Progesterone's job | What you watch |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-ovulation / breeding | Times ovulation and the breeding window | The rise: 2–3 → 8–15 ng/mL |
| Pregnancy (~63 days) | Sustains the pregnancy | The plateau: should hold >~5 ng/mL |

The transition between the two is the point most breeders miss: the marker they were using to *time* the breeding now becomes the marker they should be using to *protect* the pregnancy.

---

## What the Curve Should Look Like

In a normal pregnancy, progesterone climbs through the breeding window, stays elevated through gestation, and only falls sharply in the ~24–48 hours before whelping. The useful reference points:

- **Sustained elevation** — roughly above **5 ng/mL** — is the expected picture for most of gestation.
- **Below ~2 ng/mL** — associated with pregnancy loss; the pregnancy is in trouble.
- **A falling series** — even while still "normal" — is the earliest, most actionable warning of luteal failure.

The key insight: you are not waiting for a single "too low" reading. You are watching the *slope*. A value that was 18 ng/mL two weeks ago and is 7 ng/mL today is more alarming than a stable 6 ng/mL, even though both are technically "normal."

---

## Hypoluteoidism: The Corpus Luteum That Fails

Hypoluteoidism (luteal insufficiency) is premature failure of the corpus luteum to maintain adequate progesterone, and it is a recognised cause of pregnancy loss and resorption. The problem is that it is **silent** — the bitch looks normal right up until she resorbs the litter, by which point it is too late.

This is exactly the case serial monitoring exists for. A bitch with a history of unexplained pregnancy loss, or a falling curve on a routine check, can be caught in the window where exogenous progesterone (or a progestin) may still hold the pregnancy. Miss that window — wait for clinical signs — and the litter is already lost.

---

## Who Actually Needs Serial Testing

Serial progesterone in pregnancy is not for everyone. The honest targeting:

- **History of pregnancy loss or resorption** — the highest-yield group.
- **Older bitches** — luteal function can be less robust.
- **Breeds with known reproductive fragility** — where a lost litter is a costly event.
- **Suspected early drop** — a single low-ish value that warrants confirmation.

Screening every healthy, first-time, uneventful pregnancy dilutes the test's value. This is a targeted tool, not a routine reflex — and the distinction is what separates a useful protocol from a wasteful one.

---

## How the Test Runs

1. Once pregnancy is confirmed (relaxin ~day 28–30 or ultrasound), establish a baseline progesterone.
2. Repeat at intervals for at-risk pregnancies to watch the slope.
3. If the curve falls (especially toward ~2 ng/mL), intervene early with supplementation under veterinary guidance.
4. Confirm the pregnancy remains viable with follow-up ultrasound or relaxin.

## FAQ

### Why does progesterone matter during pregnancy?

It is the pregnancy-sustaining hormone — the corpus luteum must hold it elevated for the full ~**63-day** gestation. A sustained fall is the earliest warning of loss.

### What level is concerning?

Progesterone should hold above ~**5 ng/mL**; a fall below ~**2 ng/mL** is associated with pregnancy loss. A falling series — even "normal" — is the real red flag.

### What is hypoluteoidism?

Premature corpus-luteum failure causing pregnancy loss. It is silent until resorption, which is why a curve falling toward 2 ng/mL — not waiting for signs — is the point.

### Why serial testing?

Because the **trend is the signal**. A curve falling toward 2 ng/mL catches luteal failure days before the pregnancy is lost, when supplementation can still rescue it.

### Does every pregnant bitch need it?

No — target bitches with a history of loss, older bitches, or a suspected early drop (toward 2 ng/mL). Screening everyone dilutes the test's value.

### What if progesterone is falling?

Supplement with progesterone/progestin **early** — before the ~2 ng/mL threshold is crossed — which is why you monitor the slope rather than wait for signs.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Progesterone has two jobs** — timing at breeding, then life-support through ~**63 days** of gestation. The same marker you used to breed now protects the pregnancy.
2. **Hold above ~5, worry below ~2 ng/mL** — sustained elevation is normal; a fall toward ~**2 ng/mL** means the pregnancy is in trouble.
3. **The slope beats the single value** — a value falling from **18 → 7 ng/mL** is more alarming than a stable **6 ng/mL**, even though both read "normal."
4. **Hypoluteoidism is silent** — the corpus luteum fails before signs appear; a curve falling toward 2 ng/mL is how you catch it while supplementation can still work.
5. **Target, don't blanket** — serial testing is for at-risk pregnancies across the ~63-day gestation, not every healthy first-timer. Precision beats reflex.
