Cats are masters at hiding illness, and many feline infectious diseases produce subtle or overlapping signs. Routine, targeted testing is the clinician's most reliable tool for early detection. This guide covers the major feline infectious diseases, the tests available, and how to interpret results.

## Antigen vs. Antibody: The Foundation

Feline testing uses the same two fundamental approaches as canine testing:

- **Antigen tests** detect the pathogen directly, indicating **active infection**.
- **Antibody tests** detect the immune response, indicating **exposure or vaccination**.

For feline viruses in particular, this distinction is clinically critical. A positive FeLV antigen test means the virus is present and the cat is likely persistently infected. A positive FPV antibody test usually means the cat is protected. See [Antigen vs. Antibody Testing](/blog/canine-feline-antigen-vs-antibody-testing) for the full framework.

## Feline Panleukopenia Virus (FPV)

### The Disease
Panleukopenia — "feline distemper" — is a highly contagious parvovirus that attacks the bone marrow and intestinal lining, causing profound leukopenia, vomiting, and diarrhoea. It is frequently fatal in kittens.

### Testing
- **FPV antigen test** — detects virus in faecal or rectal-swab samples; the standard for acute diagnosis.
- **FPV antibody test** — assesses immunity from vaccination or exposure.

### Interpretation
A positive antigen test in a symptomatic cat confirms panleukopenia and demands immediate isolation. Because the virus is extremely stable in the environment, disinfection and isolation protocols are essential.

## Feline Herpesvirus (FHV-1)

### The Disease
FHV-1 causes feline viral rhinotracheitis — upper-respiratory disease with sneezing, nasal and ocular discharge, and conjunctivitis. After recovery, the virus becomes latent and can reactivate during stress.

### Testing
- **FHV-1 antigen test** — detects active viral shedding.
- **FHV-1 antibody test** — assesses exposure or immunity.

### Interpretation
Antigen testing confirms active infection during clinical episodes. Antibody testing is less commonly used for acute diagnosis but useful for population screening. See [Does My Cat Need a Feline Herpes (FHV-1) Test?](/blog/does-my-cat-should-do-the-feline-herpes-test).

## Feline Calicivirus (FCV)

### The Disease
Calicivirus causes oral ulceration, salivation, and upper-respiratory signs. A highly virulent systemic form can be severe and even fatal.

### Testing
- **FCV antigen test** — detects active infection.
- **FCV antibody test** — assesses exposure or immunity.

### Interpretation
Calicivirus and herpesvirus frequently co-infect and produce overlapping signs, so combined panels are common. See [A Feline Triple Test](/blog/a-feline-triple-test-one-simple-step-to-check-for-three-common-viruses).

## Feline Coronavirus & FIP (FCoV)

### The Disease
Feline coronavirus is common and usually causes mild or no signs. In a small percentage of cats, the virus mutates, causing feline infectious peritonitis (FIP) — a severe, historically fatal systemic disease.

### Testing
**FCoV antigen testing** detects the coronavirus. Interpretation requires care: a positive result indicates coronavirus infection, but **not** necessarily FIP. FIP diagnosis typically combines clinical signs, antibody titres, and other markers.

## Feline Leukemia Virus (FeLV)

### The Disease
FeLV is a retrovirus that suppresses the immune system, predisposing cats to secondary infections, anaemia, and lymphoma. It is a leading infectious cause of death in cats.

### Testing
**FeLV antigen testing** detects the virus itself and is the standard screening test. A positive result indicates infection (though early, transient infection can occasionally clear).

### Interpretation
FeLV status has profound implications for management, vaccination, and multi-cat households. Positive cats require lifelong monitoring and separation from negative cats. See [What Is the Feline Leukemia (FeLV) and FIV Test?](/blog/what-is-the-feline-leukemia-fiv-test).

## Feline Immunodeficiency Virus (FIV)

### The Disease
FIV, also a retrovirus, progressively weakens the immune system over years. Cats may remain asymptomatic for long periods before developing secondary infections.

### Testing
**FIV antibody testing** is the standard screening method, as antibodies persist once a cat is infected.

### Interpretation
A positive FIV antibody test indicates infection (or maternal antibodies in very young kittens). FIV-positive cats can live long, good-quality lives with proper management.

## When Should You Test?

- **New kittens or adopted cats** — screen for FeLV and FIV before introduction to a household.
- **Upper-respiratory signs** — test for FHV-1 and FCV.
- **Vomiting/diarrhoea, especially in kittens** — test for FPV.
- **Fever of unknown origin** — consider FeLV, FIV, and FCoV.
- **Before vaccination** — antibody testing can guide protocols.
- **Multi-cat facilities** — routine screening to protect the population.

## Conclusion

Feline infectious diseases are diverse, but rapid, quantitative testing brings clarity. By matching antigen vs. antibody testing to the clinical question and testing early, veterinarians can detect disease sooner, manage infectious cats appropriately, and protect the broader feline population.
