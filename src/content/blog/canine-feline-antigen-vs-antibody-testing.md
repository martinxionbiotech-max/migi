One of the most common sources of confusion in veterinary diagnostics is the difference between antigen and antibody tests. Both are essential tools, but they answer different clinical questions — and choosing the wrong one can lead to misinterpretation.

## Antigen Tests: Detecting the Pathogen

Antigen tests detect the presence of the pathogen itself — a viral or bacterial protein — in the patient's sample. Because they identify the infectious agent directly, they are the test of choice for **diagnosing active infection**.

**Antigen tests answer the question:** "Is the pathogen present right now?"

Common examples in veterinary medicine include antigen tests for canine parvovirus (CPV), feline leukemia virus (FeLV), and feline panleukopenia virus (FPV).

**When to use antigen testing:**
- Confirming acute infection in a symptomatic patient
- Screening new animals entering a shelter or cattery
- Confirming a suspected outbreak

## Antibody Tests: Detecting the Immune Response

Antibody tests detect the patient's immune response to a pathogen — the antibodies produced after infection or vaccination. They are the test of choice for **assessing immunity status**.

**Antibody tests answer the question:** "Has the animal been exposed or vaccinated, and is it protected?"

Common examples include antibody tests for canine distemper (CDV), canine parvovirus (CPV), and feline calicivirus (FCV).

**When to use antibody testing:**
- Confirming vaccine response
- Determining whether a booster is needed
- Assessing maternal antibody levels in puppies and kittens

## The Value of Quantitative Results

Traditional rapid tests provide only a qualitative positive/negative answer. Quantitative fluorescence immunoassays go further by measuring the actual concentration of antigen or antibody. This enables:

- **Antibody titre monitoring** — tracking whether protection is rising or waning over time
- **Objective treatment monitoring** — confirming that antigen levels fall with successful therapy
- **Standardised reference ranges** — reducing subjectivity in interpretation

## Putting It Together

A well-designed diagnostic strategy uses both test types appropriately:

1. **Sick patient with compatible signs** → antigen test to confirm active infection
2. **Vaccination check** → antibody test to confirm protective immunity
3. **Monitoring therapy** → serial antigen testing to track response
4. **Breeding or boarding screening** → antibody panels to assess herd immunity

## Summary

Understanding the difference between antigen and antibody testing — and when to use each — is fundamental to accurate veterinary diagnosis. Quantitative fluorescence immunoassays add a new dimension to both, providing objective, measurable results that support more confident clinical decision-making.
