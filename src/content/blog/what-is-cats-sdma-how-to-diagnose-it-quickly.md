> **TL;DR** — Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is one of the most common diseases of older cats, and the old standby — creatinine — is late to the party: it doesn't rise until **60–75% of kidney function is gone**, and it's distorted by muscle mass. SDMA fixes both problems: it rises at **25–40% loss** and ignores muscle mass, catching CKD a year or more earlier — when diet and management can still slow it down. The reference interval is 0–14 µg/dL; a *persistent* value above that is the early-warning flag.

If there's one test that should be on every senior cat's annual panel but often isn't, it's SDMA. This guide explains what it is, why it beats creatinine, and why early detection changes the whole trajectory of feline kidney disease.

---

## Why Creatinine Shows Up Late

Chronic kidney disease in cats is sneaky. By the time a cat shows signs — drinking more, losing weight, a dull coat — a meaningful chunk of kidney function is already gone. And the marker everyone has relied on for decades makes it worse:

- **Creatinine doesn't rise until roughly 60–75% of kidney function is lost.** By the time creatinine is high, the cat is most of the way to kidney failure.
- **Creatinine is muscle-mass dependent.** An aging, cachectic cat produces less creatinine, so a failing kidney can hide behind a "normal" creatinine. The thinner and sicker the cat, the less you can trust the number.

That's the trap: the test most vets order *last* to confirm kidney disease is the one that detects it *last*.

---

## What SDMA Is, and Why It's Different

**SDMA (symmetric dimethylarginine)** is a methylated amino acid produced at a constant rate by all cells and cleared almost entirely by the kidneys. Two properties make it the early-warning marker creatinine isn't:

1. **It rises early** — at 25–40% function loss, versus creatinine's 60–75%. That's often a year or more of lead time.
2. **It's muscle-mass independent** — a wasted senior cat and a muscular young cat with the same kidney function read the same SDMA. Creatinine can't make that claim.

| Marker | Rises at | Muscle-mass dependent? |
|---|---|---|
| Creatinine | ~60–75% function loss | Yes |
| SDMA | ~25–40% function loss | No |

The reference interval for cats (and dogs) is **0–14 µg/dL**. A *persistent* value above 14 µg/dL — especially with a normal creatinine — is the hallmark of **Stage 1 kidney disease**, the earliest and most treatable stage.

---

## Reading the Number Honestly

One caution, because it's where SDMA gets over-read:

A single SDMA of 15–17 µg/dL is **not** a diagnosis — it's a flag to recheck. Independent validation found that the 14 µg/dL cut-off produces false positives in a meaningful share of non-azotemic cats, and that a slightly higher threshold is more accurate. The point isn't that SDMA is unreliable — it's that, like every kidney marker, **the trend across serial measurements is the diagnosis, not one value.**

The practical rule: a single elevated SDMA means "re-test and watch." A persistent, climbing SDMA — or an SDMA that's elevated alongside a rising creatinine — is the real signal to act.

---

## Why This Matters for Older Cats Specifically

SDMA's early lead time matters most in exactly the population where CKD is common: **cats over 10.**

CKD is one of the most frequent diagnoses in senior felines, and its early signs are easy to write off — a bit more thirst, a bit less appetite, a little weight loss. That's "just getting old" until it isn't. By the time the signs are undeniable, the disease is advanced and the options are narrower.

SDMA on the senior panel changes that math: it catches the decline while the cat still looks and acts mostly normal, at the point where a renal diet, blood-pressure control and monitoring can genuinely slow progression and extend good-quality time. That's the entire value proposition — not earlier *diagnosis* for its own sake, but earlier *intervention* while it still moves the needle.

## Where SDMA Fits in the Workup

SDMA doesn't replace creatinine — it pairs with it:

| Scenario | What to do |
|---|---|
| Routine senior-cat screening | SDMA + creatinine on the panel |
| Normal creatinine, elevated SDMA (persistent) | Stage 1 CKD — start management, re-check serially |
| Borderline SDMA (15–17) | Re-check; don't over-interpret a single value |

## FAQ

### What is SDMA in cats?

A kidney-function marker that rises much earlier than creatinine — at 25–40% function loss versus 60–75% — catching kidney disease at its earliest, most treatable stage.

### Why does creatinine miss early kidney disease?

It only rises after most function is gone and is affected by muscle mass, so a thin aging cat can have significant disease with a normal creatinine. SDMA rises earlier and ignores muscle mass.

### What's a normal SDMA level for a cat?

0–14 µg/dL. A persistent value above 14 µg/dL with normal creatinine supports Stage 1 kidney disease — but recheck a single borderline reading.

### Why screen senior cats with SDMA?

CKD is one of the most common diseases of cats over 10, and its early signs are easy to miss. SDMA catches decline a year or more before creatinine.

### Is SDMA affected by muscle mass or diet?

No — it's largely independent of both, which is why it's more reliable than creatinine for early detection in aging cats.

1. **Creatinine is late** — it waits until 60–75% of kidney function is gone and is distorted by muscle mass.
2. **SDMA rises at 25–40% loss** — often a year or more of lead time, independent of muscle mass.
3. **0–14 µg/dL is the reference interval** — a persistent elevation with normal creatinine is Stage 1 CKD.
4. **The trend is the diagnosis** — a single borderline value is a flag to recheck, not a verdict.
