> **TL;DR** — The analyzer price is the sticker; **reagent TCO** is the real cost, and they are not the same number. The per-test figure that matters is not the reagent sticker — it is the reagent plus **QC** (a control run consumes reagent), **calibrators**, **failed and repeated runs** (every retest is a second reagent), and **shelf-life waste** (a 10-test box opened for one patient, with the rest expiring, has a true cost per test ten times the sticker). The calculation is simple and the trap is hidden in it: divide the *total* spend by the number of **reported** tests, not purchased tests. The gap between those two denominators is where most of the real expense lives — and it is exactly the gap a sticker price never shows you.

---

## In Plain Terms

Buying a diagnostic test is not like buying a pen. You pay for the reagent, yes — but also for the control you run to make sure it works, the calibrator, the test you had to redo because the sample was hemolysed, and the nine tests left in the box that expired because you only needed one. The sticker price is the tip of the iceberg.

---

## What TCO Actually Includes

| Cost component | What it is | Why it hides |
|---|---|---|
| Reagent | The test itself | Visible, but not the whole story |
| QC | Control runs | Consumes reagent, easy to forget |
| Calibrators | Calibration materials | Recurring on triggers |
| Failed/repeat runs | Retests | Every retest is a second reagent |
| Shelf-life waste | Expired unused tests | Huge for low-volume practices |
| Analyzer amortisation | Capital spread over time | Often excluded from "per-test" |

The last one matters too: the analyzer is a capital cost that should be amortised into the per-test figure over its life. Ignore it and your "per-test cost" is flattering but wrong.

---

## The Shelf-Life Trap

This is the biggest hidden cost for a low-volume practice. A reagent box is commonly a **10-test** box with a **2-year** shelf life unopened — but once opened, the stability window shortens. Run two tests a month and that box may outlive its usefulness before you finish it.

The arithmetic is brutal: buy a 10-test box, use one test, let nine expire, and your *effective* cost per reported test is **ten times** the per-test sticker. Matching box size to your actual volume is not a detail — it is the difference between a profitable test and a money-losing one.

---

## The Real Per-Test Formula

The honest calculation is:

**True cost per reported test = (reagent cost + QC cost + calibrator cost + repeat cost + waste) ÷ number of reported tests**

The key word is **reported**. You pay for *purchased* tests; you only get paid for *reported* tests. The gap — the tests you bought but could not report — is pure loss, and it is driven by QC consumption, failures, and expiry.

---

## Why the Analyzer Price Misleads

A common buying error is to compare analyzers on purchase price while ignoring the reagent stream. That is backwards: the analyzer is a one-time cost, while the reagent cost recurs with **every test for the life of the instrument**. Over three years, a slightly cheaper analyzer paired with high-waste or high-priced reagents can cost far more than a pricier analyzer with efficient consumables.

The discipline is to evaluate the **full three-year TCO** — analyzer plus expected reagent, QC, calibration and waste — not the sticker on either side.

---

## What to Ask a Supplier

When a supplier quotes you a price, get the whole picture:

1. **Per-test reagent price** — and the box size (does it match your volume?).
2. **QC and calibrator consumption** — how many control runs does the schedule require?
3. **Stability** — on-board and opened-box, and what happens to unused tests.
4. **Failure/repeat rate** — what should you budget for retests?
5. **Analyzer amortisation** — what is the capital cost spread over its life?

A supplier who only gives you the reagent sticker is not answering the question you actually asked — which is "what will this test cost me per reported result, month after month?"

---

## How the Test Runs

1. Estimate your monthly volume per analyte.
2. Add up reagent + QC + calibrator + repeat + waste costs.
3. Divide by the number of *reported* tests.
4. Compare that true figure across suppliers — not the sticker.

## FAQ

### What is TCO for a diagnostic test?

The full cost over time — reagent + QC + calibrators + failed runs + shelf-life waste + amortised analyzer, not just the sticker (a 10-test box can cost 10× if wasted).

### Why per-test cost over analyzer price?

The analyzer is one-time; reagents recur with **every test for life**. TCO, not sticker, is what you actually pay over 3 years.

### What hidden costs inflate per-test?

QC, calibrators, failed/repeat runs, and shelf-life waste — a **10-test** box opened for one patient can cost **10×** the sticker per reported test.

### How does shelf life affect cost?

A **2-year** shelf life and a shorter opened-box window mean low-volume practices waste tests. Match box size to volume.

### How to calculate true cost per test?

(Total spend) ÷ (**reported** tests, not purchased) — 2 denominators, and the gap between them is the hidden loss.

### What to ask a supplier?

Full TCO: per-test price, QC/calibrator consumption, a 2-year shelf life, failure rate, and amortisation — not just the sticker.

## Key Takeaways

1. **The sticker is the tip of the iceberg** — TCO includes QC, calibrators, repeats and waste; a 10-test box used once can cost 10× per reported test.
2. **Reagent cost is recurring, the analyzer is one-time** — over 3 years, reagent TCO dominates the analyzer price.
3. **Shelf-life waste is the silent killer** — a **10-test** box used once can cost **10×** per reported test when the rest expire.
4. **Divide by reported tests, not purchased** — 2 denominators, and the gap between them is where the real expense hides.
5. **Ask for the full picture** — per-test price, QC/calibrator consumption, a 2-year shelf life, failure rate. A sticker-only quote is not an answer.
