> **TL;DR** — Canine infectious disease shows up as **3 shared signs** — fever, vomiting, diarrhoea — so testing is what names the culprit. The framework is always **2 tests, 2 questions**: antigen detects the pathogen (active infection), antibody detects immunity (vaccination or exposure). At least **4 viral culprits** — parvo, distemper, corona and adenovirus — each need a different test, and the one you pick depends on whether the dog is sick or being screened for protection.

Infectious disease is among the most common reasons dogs present to veterinary clinics, yet the signs are frequently vague — fever, lethargy, vomiting, diarrhoea — and shared across many diseases. Targeted, rapid testing is therefore essential to identify the causative agent and guide treatment. This guide covers the major canine infectious diseases, the tests used to detect them, and how to interpret results.

## The Test Landscape: Antigen vs. Antibody

Before examining individual diseases, it is essential to understand the two fundamental testing approaches.

- **Antigen tests** detect the **pathogen itself** — a viral or bacterial protein — in the patient's sample. They indicate **active infection** and are the preferred test for acute diagnosis.
- **Antibody tests** detect the **host's immune response** — immunoglobulins produced against the pathogen. They indicate **exposure or vaccination**, not necessarily active disease.

The distinction matters enormously in interpretation. A puppy with diarrhoea and a positive CPV antigen test has active parvovirus. A healthy adult dog with CPV antibodies has simply been vaccinated or previously exposed. For a full treatment of this topic, see [Antigen vs. Antibody Testing in Veterinary Diagnostics](/blog/canine-feline-antigen-vs-antibody-testing).

## Canine Parvovirus (CPV)

### The Disease
Canine parvovirus causes severe, often fatal gastroenteritis, primarily in puppies and unvaccinated adults. The virus attacks rapidly dividing cells in the intestinal lining and bone marrow, causing haemorrhagic diarrhoea, vomiting, and profound immunosuppression.

### Testing
- **CPV antigen test** — detects viral antigen in faecal or rectal-swab samples. It is the standard rapid test for acute infection and can be positive within days of exposure.
- **CPV antibody test** — measures vaccine response or exposure; useful for assessing immunity and vaccination protocols.

### Interpretation
A positive antigen test in a symptomatic dog confirms parvovirus and warrants immediate isolation and intensive supportive care. A negative result in a strongly symptomatic puppy should be interpreted cautiously — very early or very late in the disease, viral shedding may be intermittent.

## Canine Distemper Virus (CDV)

### The Disease
Distemper is a highly contagious, frequently fatal virus affecting the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and nervous systems. Early signs include fever, ocular and nasal discharge, and cough, progressing to neurological signs such as seizures and myoclonus.

### Testing
- **CDV antigen test** — detects viral antigen, useful in the early, viraemic phase.
- **CDV antibody test** — measures immunity from vaccination or exposure; commonly used to verify vaccine response.

### Interpretation
Distemper is challenging to diagnose on clinical signs alone because early signs mimic kennel cough. Antigen testing aids early detection; antibody testing supports vaccination-programme management.

## Canine Coronavirus (CCV)

### The Disease
Canine coronavirus causes gastroenteritis, usually milder than parvovirus but potentially severe in puppies or when co-infecting with CPV. Signs include vomiting and diarrhoea.

### Testing
**CCV antigen testing** detects the virus in faecal samples. Because signs overlap with parvovirus, the two are often tested together.

## Canine Adenovirus (CAV)

### The Disease
Canine adenovirus type 1 causes infectious canine hepatitis; type 2 contributes to respiratory disease (kennel cough). Vaccination has made severe disease less common, but testing remains valuable for diagnosis and immunity assessment.

### Testing
**CAV antibody testing** assesses vaccine response and immunity status. Combined panels (CDV/CPV/CAV) are convenient for verifying core-vaccine immunity.

## Leishmaniasis (Leishmania)

### The Disease
Canine leishmaniasis, caused by the protozoan *Leishmania infantum* and transmitted by sandflies, is a serious systemic disease endemic in the Mediterranean basin, South America, and parts of Asia. Signs include skin lesions, weight loss, lymphadenopathy, and renal failure.

### Testing
**Leishmania antibody testing** is the primary screening method. Quantitative antibody levels help stage disease and monitor treatment response.

**Related product:** [Canine Leishmania Antibody Test](/reagents/migi-dog-canine-leishmania-antibody-clsh-ab-rapid-quantitative-test-kit)

## Toxoplasmosis (Toxoplasma gondii)

### The Disease
*Toxoplasma gondii* is a protozoan that can cause neurological, ocular, and systemic disease, and is a zoonotic concern. Dogs may be subclinical carriers or show signs depending on immune status.

### Testing
**Toxoplasma antibody testing** detects exposure. Interpretation must consider that antibody presence indicates exposure, not necessarily active disease.

## When Should You Test?

- **Acute gastrointestinal signs** — test for CPV and CCV immediately.
- **Respiratory signs** — consider CDV and CAV.
- **Neurological signs** — consider CDV and toxoplasmosis.
- **Skin lesions or systemic disease** — consider leishmaniasis (especially in endemic regions).
- **Pre-breeding or pre-travel screening** — antibody panels to confirm immunity.

## Choosing the Right Test

The choice between antigen and antibody testing depends on the clinical question:

- **"Is the dog infected right now?"** → antigen test.
- **"Is the dog protected?"** → antibody test.
- **"Has the dog been exposed?"** → antibody test.

When in doubt, testing both can clarify the picture: a positive antigen test with rising antibodies indicates an active infection in progress.

## Conclusion

Rapid, quantitative testing has made canine infectious-disease diagnosis faster and more precise than ever. By matching the right test to the clinical question — antigen for active infection, antibody for immunity — and interpreting results in context, veterinary teams can intervene earlier and improve outcomes.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Vague signs, specific tests** — 3 shared signs (fever, vomiting, diarrhoea) hide very different diseases, so testing names the cause.
2. **Antigen = active infection, antibody = immunity** — the 2-question framework applies to every canine virus.
3. **Parvo and distemper are the 2 urgent ones** — early antigen testing changes the outcome; corona and adenovirus can wait.
4. **Match the test to the question** — 1 sick dog gets an antigen test; 1 healthy dog gets an antibody test.
5. **One panel covers 4 common culprits** — parvo, distemper, corona and adenovirus, with Leishmania and Toxoplasma added by risk.

## FAQ

### What does canine infectious-disease testing cover?

Four major canine viral pathogens — parvo, distemper, coronavirus and adenovirus — using 2 test types: antigen tests for active infection and antibody tests for immunity.

### Which test should I use for a sick dog?

One choice for a sick dog: an antigen test, because it detects the pathogen directly and confirms active infection — which is what a symptomatic dog needs.

### Which test checks protection?

One choice for protection checks: an antibody test, because it measures the immune response from vaccination or prior exposure.

### Why test instead of treating on signs?

Because 3 clinical signs overlap heavily, while treatment, isolation and prognosis differ by disease — a specific test prevents both missed and wrong diagnoses.
