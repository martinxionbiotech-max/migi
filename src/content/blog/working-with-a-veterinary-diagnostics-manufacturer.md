## Why This Guide Matters

Here's a fact that should shape every buying decision in this space: the global veterinary diagnostics market was worth roughly **USD 10.85 billion in 2024** and is projected to reach **USD 28.27 billion by 2034**, growing at a **10.05% CAGR** (Nova One Advisor). Consumables — reagents and kits, exactly what a POCT manufacturer sells — account for **more than half** of that market. Companion animals (dogs and cats) drive ~59% of revenue.

That growth attracts a lot of suppliers. And in a fast-growing market, the difference between a good manufacturer and a mediocre one isn't visible on a spec sheet — it shows up six months later, when a batch drifts, a shipment is late, or a cartridge lot fails QC.

This guide is for the buyer who has to live with those consequences: the distributor, the clinic group, the importer, the private-label brand. It's written to help you evaluate a factory the way a professional would — on process, not promises.

## In Plain Terms

Buying from a diagnostics manufacturer is a bit like hiring a contractor to build houses you'll then sell under your own name. You don't just look at one finished house — you look at whether they have a repeatable process, whether they'll still be there to fix the roof in year three, and whether they'll let you put your own sign on the front lawn. A pretty prototype is easy. Consistent, certifiable, ongoing production is the hard part — and that's what actually protects your business.

## The Five Pillars of Manufacturer Evaluation

Every serious evaluation reduces to five dimensions. Miss any one, and the deal will hurt you later.

### 1. Product Performance

This is table stakes, but dig past the marketing. For a quantitative immunofluorescence system, ask for the *actual* performance numbers:

| Attribute | What to Ask For |
|---|---|
| Sensitivity | Detection limit in pg/mL or ng/mL per analyte |
| Precision | CV% (coefficient of variation) — ideally <10% |
| Dynamic range | Linear range of the assay |
| Menu breadth | How many validated analytes, and which species |
| Sample types | Whole blood, serum, plasma compatibility |

A manufacturer that won't share CV% or detection limits — or that gives you only marketing language — is a red flag. Reputable factories publish this data.

### 2. Certifications and Quality System

Certifications are the shortcut to trust. They tell you a third party has audited the factory's processes, not just its marketing.

- **ISO 9001** — the baseline quality-management standard. Table stakes for export.
- **ISO 13485** — medical-device-specific QMS. Stronger, and increasingly expected for diagnostic reagents.
- **CE marking (EU IVDR)** — legally required to sell in the EU. The IVDR (Regulation 2017/746) tightened requirements significantly.
- **FDA CVM** — in the US, veterinary diagnostics are regulated by the FDA's Center for Veterinary Medicine, a different pathway from human IVDs.

The full landscape is covered in [Veterinary Diagnostic Certifications: CE, ISO, and QC Explained](/blog/veterinary-diagnostic-certifications-ce-iso-qc).

### 3. Manufacturing Quality

Certifications say the system *exists*; manufacturing quality is whether it *works*. Ask about:

- **Batch traceability** — can the factory trace any kit back to its raw-material lot? If there's a recall, this is what saves you.
- **Stability** — do reagents store at room temperature, or do some require cold chain? Room-temperature storage dramatically lowers your logistics cost and spoilage risk.
- **QC release testing** — is every lot tested against reference materials before shipping?

### 4. OEM / ODM Flexibility

If you're building a brand — not just reselling — this is where the value lives. Can the factory:

- Put **your brand** on the analyzer and kits?
- Customize the **reagent menu** (add or remove analytes)?
- Provide **private-label packaging**, manuals in your language, and localized software?

The full OEM/ODM path is covered in [Veterinary Diagnostic OEM/ODM: What You Need to Know](/blog/veterinary-diagnostic-oem-odm).

### 5. Post-Sale Support

The deal doesn't end at delivery. You need:

- Technical training for your team or your customers
- Warranty and repair/replacement turnaround
- **Supply continuity** — what happens when you reorder? Is there a guaranteed lead time?
- Regulatory support — documentation for your market registration

A manufacturer that goes silent after the invoice is a liability, no matter how good the product was on day one.

## The Supplier Qualification Checklist

Use this as your scorecard when you shortlist manufacturers. Score each 1–5.

| # | Check | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Shares CV% and detection limits | Performance is real, not claimed |
| 2 | ISO 9001 + ISO 13485 in scope for these products | Third-party audited QMS |
| 3 | CE IVDR for target market | Legal access to EU |
| 4 | Batch traceability to raw material | Recall protection |
| 5 | Room-temperature reagent storage | Lower logistics cost, less spoilage |
| 6 | Private label / OEM experience | You can build a brand |
| 7 | Export track record | They understand international logistics & regs |
| 8 | Documented lead times | You can plan inventory |
| 9 | Technical support in your language | Training and troubleshooting |
| 10 | References / existing distributors | Social proof of reliability |

A manufacturer that scores well on all ten is rare — but the ones that do are the ones worth building a long-term relationship with.

## The Buying Process, Step by Step

Here's what a typical engagement looks like, from inquiry to steady reorders:

1. **Inquiry & needs definition.** You define your target market, menu, volume, and brand strategy.
2. **Sample evaluation.** Get the analyzer and a starter set of kits. Run your own validation.
3. **Commercial terms.** Negotiate MOQ, pricing tiers, payment terms, and lead times.
4. **Customization** (if OEM/ODM). Branding, packaging, menu, manual localization.
5. **Regulatory registration.** CE/registration in your market, using the factory's technical file.
6. **First production order.** Pilot batch, then scale.
7. **Reordering & support.** This is where the relationship pays off — or falls apart.

The cost side of this — bulk pricing, MOQ, and distributor margins — is covered in [Bulk Ordering & Distributor Pricing: How It Works](/blog/bulk-ordering-distributor-pricing-veterinary-diagnostics).

## FAQ

**How do I verify a manufacturer's claims?**

Ask for the technical file, the certificate scope (not just the certificate), a trial batch to validate yourself, and references from existing distributors. Any manufacturer that hesitates on the first two is a warning sign.

**What's more important — analyzer price or reagent cost?**

Reagent cost. The analyzer is a one-time purchase; reagents are a recurring revenue stream. A cheap analyzer that locks you into expensive kits is a worse deal than a pricier analyzer with competitive kit pricing.

**Can I start with a small order?**

Yes. Most manufacturers offer a small trial order or distributor starter pack. Use it to validate product quality and the manufacturer's responsiveness before committing to volume.

**What does "lock-in" mean and is it a problem?**

Lock-in means the analyzer only runs the manufacturer's own cartridges (which is normal for quantitative immunoassay systems). It's fine — as long as the reagent supply is reliable and competitively priced. That's why supply continuity is on the checklist.

**How do I evaluate total cost of ownership?**

Add analyzer cost + expected reagent spend over 2–3 years + shipping + customs + spoilage (cold-chain items spoil more) + support. A slightly higher unit price with room-temperature storage and reliable supply often wins on TCO.
