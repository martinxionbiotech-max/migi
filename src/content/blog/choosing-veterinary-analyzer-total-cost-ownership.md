> **TL;DR:** Choosing an analyzer on sticker price is the fastest way to make an expensive mistake. Compare instruments on **total cost of ownership over 3 years** — hardware, cartridges, QC, maintenance, training and wastage — and price a **2-week repair turnaround** as a cost, not an inconvenience. For a targeted, time-sensitive menu, a dedicated **single-parameter immunoassay reader** is frequently the lowest-TCO choice precisely because it does **1 thing cheaply**.

Every analyzer looks good in a brochure. The differences that matter show up months later, in the cartridge invoices and the expired stock on the shelf. This checklist is the questions to ask before you sign anything — organised by the cost categories that actually decide TCO.

## 1. Hardware

- What is the **acquisition price**?
- Is there a **lease, placement, or subscription option**, and what does it cost over three years?
- What is the **warranty** period and what does it cover?
- Is the instrument **single-parameter or panel-based**, and does that match my workflow?

A dedicated fluorescence immunoassay (FIA) reader is a cartridge-based, single-parameter instrument with a deliberately low hardware cost. That low cost is its main financial virtue: it shortens payback and reduces risk. Don't let a vendor up-sell you from a single-parameter reader to a full platform unless you can prove you need the panels — and "you might grow into it" is not proof.

## 2. Consumables

- What is the **cost per cartridge**, and does it vary by test?
- Can I buy **individual test cartridges**, or am I forced into panels?
- What is the **shelf life**, and how does it affect my ordering?
- What is the **minimum order quantity**?

The cost-per-test question is more important than the hardware question, because consumables repeat for the life of the instrument. If you mostly run targeted single-parameter tests — a progesterone for breeding, a cPL for pancreatitis — single-test cartridges are the economical choice, because you never pay for unrequested analytes.

## 3. Quality Control and Calibration

- Does the instrument require **periodic calibration**? How often, and with what materials?
- What **internal quality control** does it run, and is it automated?
- What is the **cost of QC materials** per month?

This category matters more for a quantitative reader than for a rapid strip, because the number *is* the diagnosis. Prefer an analyzer with built-in, low-burden quality control — automated internal QC that doesn't consume technician time is worth a premium — and budget for whatever control materials it requires.

## 4. Maintenance and Support

- Is there a **maintenance contract**, and what does it cost annually?
- What happens **when it breaks** — is there a defined repair path and turnaround?
- How often are **software updates** released, and are they included?
- What is the **vendor's support response time** in my region?

A cartridge-based reader with no fluidics has genuinely fewer failure points than a full chemistry platform, which reduces this entire category. But confirm the vendor has a support presence in your region before committing. A cheap analyzer with no local service becomes expensive the first time it's down for two weeks.

## 5. Training and Workflow

- How many **steps** does a technician complete to get a valid result?
- Does the instrument **reject invalid samples** rather than report a wrong number?
- How long does **new-staff training** take?
- Does it integrate with my **practice management software**?

Every step is an opportunity for error, and every error is a wasted cartridge and a re-run. A workflow that is fast, forgiving and hard to get wrong is a financial asset, not a convenience.

## 6. Reagent Wastage Risk

- How precisely can I **order to demand**?
- What is the **cost of an expired cartridge**?
- Can I **re-order in small, frequent batches**?

Wastage is the hidden cost that silently destroys ROI. A vendor that lets you re-order small quantities frequently is worth more to you than a marginal discount that forces bulk purchases you may never use.

## 7. The TCO Scorecard

Bring the answers together into a three-year total:

```
TCO (3 years) =
  hardware
  + (cartridge cost × tests per year × 3)
  + (annual QC + maintenance + training) × 3
  + estimated wastage
```

Then divide by the total number of tests over three years to get a **true cost per test**. Compare instruments on that number, not on the purchase price.

## The Decision Pattern Worth Internalising

There's a recurring theme across all seven categories: the cheapest analyzer is rarely the cheapest to own, and the most expensive is rarely the best. The instruments that win on TCO are the ones whose design matches your actual workflow.

For a clinic whose needs are targeted and time-sensitive — reproductive timing, pancreatitis, cardiac and kidney biomarkers — a dedicated single-parameter fluorescence immunoassay reader is frequently the lowest-TCO choice. Not because it's the most powerful, but because it does one thing well and cheaply, with the fewest moving parts to maintain and the least capital at risk.

## Application & Commercial Angle

Who should care: clinic owners, practice managers, and anyone comparing analyzers before signing. This is a three-year cash-flow decision, not a sticker-price decision.

The practical worth is avoiding a workflow mismatch. A lower-TCO, targeted FIA reader often beats a larger platform when the menu and volume are specific — so lock the choice to fully loaded cost per test, service response and menu fit.

## FAQ

### What should I look at beyond the analyzer's sticker price?

Evaluate **1 cartridge cost per test**, QC and maintenance burden, staff training, and reagent expiry risk. Sum those recurring costs over **3 years** — they usually matter more than the 1-time hardware price.

### What makes an analyzer easy to use?

A **3-step workflow** with minimal sample preparation, automatic rejection of invalid samples, and clear quantitative readouts. Fewer steps mean fewer operator errors, less training, and less cartridge wastage.

### How do I avoid buying more analyzer than I need?

Match the instrument to the **1 menu you'll actually run most often**. A dedicated single-parameter immunoassay reader is the economical choice for targeted quantitative biomarkers, whereas a full chemistry platform only makes sense when you need broad, high-volume panels.

### Is a subscription or placement model worth it?

It depends on the numbers over **3 years**. A subscription lowers upfront cost but locks in recurring fees that can exceed the purchase price; a placement model may require a minimum test commitment. Calculate the 3-year total for each option before choosing.

### What should I confirm about the vendor before buying?

Confirm **2 things before buying**: regional support presence/response time, and the repair path/turnaround. Then verify how updates are delivered, cartridge shelf life, minimum order quantity, and whether individual cartridges are sold or only panels.

### How do I compare two analyzers fairly?

Compute the **fully loaded cost per test** for each — hardware amortised plus cartridge cost plus allocated overhead, all divided by your realistic **3-year test volume**. Compare on that number, not on the purchase price.

## Key Takeaways

1. Compare analyzers on **3-year total cost of ownership**, never on sticker price.
2. Consumables matter more than hardware — **1 cartridge reorder** repeats for the instrument's life.
3. Quantitative readers carry a real QC burden — prefer **1 automated internal QC** over manual steps.
4. Vendor support and a **2-week repair turnaround** are part of the cost, not a nice-to-have.
5. A **1-parameter FIA reader** is often the lowest-TCO choice for targeted, time-sensitive menus.
