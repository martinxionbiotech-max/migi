> **TL;DR** — Pancreatitis is the same organ failing in two very different ways. In the **dog** it announces itself — vomiting, a painful belly, the "praying" posture — and the test is **cPL** (canine pancreatic lipase). In the **cat** it hides — vague lethargy and anorexia, vomiting in only a minority — and the test is **fPL** (feline pancreatic lipase). The tests are species-specific and not interchangeable, which is why the old generic amylase/lipase are largely obsolete. The reason feline pancreatitis is under-diagnosed is not that the test is hard — it is that the signs are so vague the test is never ordered. A quantitative cPL/fPL, not a yes/no line, is what lets you track recovery and catch the relapse.

---

## In Plain Terms

The pancreas makes digestive enzymes, and in pancreatitis those enzymes activate early and start digesting the pancreas itself. The disease is the same, but the patient's behaviour could not be more different: a dog will tell you it hurts, a cat will quietly stop eating. That one behavioural difference drives almost everything about how the two species are tested.

---

## The Species Split

| | Dog | Cat |
|---|---|---|
| Test | **cPL** (canine pancreatic lipase) | **fPL** (feline pancreatic lipase) |
| Classic signs | Vomiting, abdominal pain, "praying" posture | Lethargy, anorexia, hypothermia |
| Vomiting | Common | Minority only |
| Ease of diagnosis | Moderate (signs point the way) | Hard (signs are vague) |
| Concurrent disease | Less common | Often "triaditis" (pancreas + gut + bile duct) |

The headline: **the dog's signs point to the pancreas; the cat's do not.** That is why canine pancreatitis is caught on clinical suspicion and feline pancreatitis is caught only if someone remembers to test for it.

---

## Why the Old Amylase/Lipase Tests Fail

For years, pancreatitis was tested with serum amylase and a generic lipase. Both are now understood to be poor tests:

- **Non-specific** — they rise in kidney disease, GI disease, and other non-pancreatic conditions.
- **Insensitive** — they miss a large share of genuine pancreatitis cases.

The modern standard is the **species-specific pancreatic lipase** — cPL for dogs, fPL for cats — which quantifies the enzyme that actually leaks from the damaged pancreas. It is more sensitive, more specific, and — because it is quantitative — useful for monitoring, not just diagnosing.

---

## Feline "Triaditis": Why Cats Are Extra Hard

Cats carry a compounding problem the dog does not: feline pancreatitis frequently co-occurs with inflammatory bowel disease and cholangitis, a combination called **triaditis**. When three organ systems are inflamed at once, the clinical picture blurs, and no single sign points cleanly at the pancreas.

The consequence is practical: in a vague, inappetent cat, pancreatitis should be on the differential list *by default*, with a specific fPL test to confirm or exclude it. The test is the tool that cuts through the ambiguity that the signs cannot resolve.

---

## Quantitative Matters for Monitoring

Both cPL and fPL can be run as a quantitative immunoassay, and the number is what makes them monitoring tools rather than one-off diagnostics:

- **Falling value** → the inflammation is resolving; treatment is working.
- **Rising value** → worsening or relapse; escalate.

Because the result is a stored number, you can compare today's value against the patient's last one — the same logic as tracking SDMA or NT-proBNP. A yes/no line cannot do this; only a number can.

## FAQ

### Why separate tests for dogs and cats?

Because pancreatic lipase is **species-specific** — cPL for dogs, fPL for cats, and they are not interchangeable. The old generic amylase/lipase are largely obsolete — use the 2 species-specific tests.

### How do the signs differ?

Dogs **announce** it (vomiting, pain, "praying" posture); cats **hide** it (lethargy, anorexia, vomiting in a minority, unlike dogs where it is common). That is why feline disease is under-diagnosed — 2 species, 2 presentations.

### Why is feline pancreatitis harder?

Vague signs plus frequent **triaditis** (3 organs: pancreas + IBD + cholangitis) blur the picture. A specific fPL test is what cuts through it.

### Are amylase and lipase still useful?

Largely no — non-specific and insensitive. The 2 species-specific tests **cPL/fPL** are the modern standard.

### cPL vs fPL testing?

Both quantify pancreatic lipase, tuned to each of the 2 species; run as a **quantitative** immunoassay so the number can track severity and recovery.

### How is it monitored?

Serial quantitative cPL/fPL — a **falling** value tracks recovery, a **rising** one signals relapse; 2 points make the trend.

## Key Takeaways

1. **2 species, 2 tests** — cPL for dogs, fPL for cats; species-specific and not interchangeable. Generic amylase/lipase are obsolete.
2. **Dogs announce, cats hide** — vomiting and pain in the dog, vague lethargy in the cat (vomiting in a minority). Feline pancreatitis is under-diagnosed because the test is never ordered — 2 species, 2 clinical faces.
3. **Triaditis muddies the cat** — 3 organs inflamed at once (pancreas + IBD + cholangitis) blur the picture; keep pancreatitis on the feline differential by default.
4. **Quantitative beats a line** — a stored number tracks recovery (falling) and relapse (rising), which a yes/no strip cannot. 2 serial values, not a line, let you grade severity.
5. **The test is easy; remembering it is the hard part** — in a vague cat, order fPL (one of 2 species-specific lipase tests) before the disease is missed.
