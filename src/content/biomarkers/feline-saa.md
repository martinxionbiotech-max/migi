## Quick Answer

Serum amyloid A is the **dominant** acute-phase protein in the cat, and that single fact is the reason fSAA deserves a page of its own rather than being treated as "the feline version of CRP". In dogs, CRP is the major acute-phase responder; in cats the hepatic response is carried mainly by SAA, while CRP rises modestly. Measuring canine-style markers in cats and interpreting them with canine expectations is a common and quiet source of false reassurance.

## What fSAA measures

SAA is an apolipoprotein produced by the liver under interleukin-1 and interleukin-6 stimulation. It associates with high-density lipoprotein in plasma, rises within hours of an inflammatory stimulus, and falls quickly once the stimulus resolves — faster than immunoglobulins and, in cats, with a larger dynamic range than CRP.

Two consequences follow:

1. **It is a magnitude signal with a short memory.** A high value means something is happening now or very recently, not that something happened last month.
2. **It is not a localization signal.** SAA says "inflammation of this magnitude"; it does not say where, and it does not distinguish infectious from sterile inflammation.

## Why quantitative matters more in cats than in dogs

Cats are masters of the unremarkable examination. A cat with pancreatitis, cholangitis, or a focal abscess may present with nothing more specific than anorexia and lethargy. A qualitative strip that reports "elevated" collapses mild, moderate and severe into one band, at exactly the point where the clinician is trying to decide whether to hospitalise, image, or send home with symptomatic care.

A quantitative value supports three things a strip cannot:

| Question | Strip | Quantitative fSAA |
|---|---|---|
| Is there a systemic inflammatory response? | Yes/no above cut-off | Magnitude relative to the platform interval |
| Is it resolving on therapy? | Only after it drops below the cut-off | Change between two dates against assay imprecision |
| Is this cat's value plausible for the clinical picture? | Not assessable | Comparable with the platform's own reference interval |

The third row is underrated. Quantitative values let a clinician notice discordance — a severely ill cat with a normal SAA, or a bright cat with a markedly elevated one — and discordance is often the most informative finding of the visit.

## Where fSAA changes decisions

- **Feline pancreatitis.** SAA is commonly elevated, but it is not specific to the pancreas; pairing with fPL separates "pancreatitis" from "pancreatitis plus systemic inflammation", which changes the treatment plan.
- **Post-operative monitoring.** A value that fails to fall after surgery suggests a complication earlier than clinical signs do.
- **Unexplained anorexia or fever.** A normal SAA in a genuinely unwell cat shifts the differential away from systemic inflammation and towards non-inflammatory causes — useful negative information.
- **Cholangitis and triaditis.** SAA tracks the inflammatory component and responds to therapy.
- **Neoplasia.** Some tumours produce an acute-phase response; a persistently elevated SAA with no infectious focus deserves imaging.

## Six interpretation traps

1. **Using CRP as the feline acute-phase marker.** In cats, SAA carries the response; CRP is a weaker signal.
2. **Reading a single value as a diagnosis.** It is a magnitude, not a disease.
3. **Assuming a normal value excludes disease.** Wall-localized, chronic low-grade, and central nervous system inflammation can sit inside the interval.
4. **Comparing platforms.** Method and antibody differences produce proportional bias; a value from another system is not transferable.
5. **Ignoring the sampling interval.** A re-check drawn too soon may sit inside the assay's imprecision band and read as "no change".
6. **Treating SAA as an antibiotic decision tool.** It answers whether inflammation is present and whether therapy is working, not which organism is involved.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** Feline SAA is the strongest clinical differentiator in this menu, and it is usually marketed as the weaker sibling of CRP — the reverse of its actual value.

**Reasoning.** Canine CRP is a crowded space: every POCT vendor offers something, the literature is thick, and the buyer can compare freely. Feline SAA is narrower: fewer platforms offer a feline-specific quantitative assay, feline acute-phase biology genuinely differs from canine, and the clinical question it answers — "is this cat systemically inflamed?" — is asked constantly in feline practice where examination findings are thin. A vendor that positions fSAA as a feline-specific tool, with feline reference intervals and feline decision bands, occupies ground that canine-CRP-led competitors have not claimed. Positioning it as "fSAA, our cat CRP" gives that ground away.
