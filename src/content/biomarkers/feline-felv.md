## Quick Answer

FeLV testing looks simple and is not: the virus produces several distinct outcomes — abortive exposure, regressive infection, progressive infection and focal infection — and an antigen test detects only the antigenaemic phase. That is why a single positive result should be confirmed, and why a negative result in a cat with recent exposure risk should be repeated. The practical rule is: test, confirm, and where suspicion persists, retest rather than assume.

## What the test measures

The standard screen detects FeLV p27 antigen in serum or plasma, which indicates circulating viral antigen. It is not an antibody test, and that is deliberate: antibody is uninformative in vaccinated cats and does not distinguish protective immunity from infection.

| Outcome | Antigen | Provirus (PCR) | Clinical meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Abortive | Negative | Negative | Virus cleared before establishment; immune, not infected |
| Regressive | Transient positive, then negative | Positive | Virus integrated but controlled; low risk of shedding |
| Progressive | Persistently positive | Positive | Active infection; shedding; disease risk |
| Focal | Negative or intermittent | Positive in tissue | Atypical; sanctuary sites such as mammary tissue |

The regressive category is the one most often missed by a single test: a cat can be transiently antigen positive and then become negative while remaining provirus positive.

## Why confirmation matters

A positive antigen result in a low-risk cat — an indoor single cat with no exposure history — has a meaningful chance of being false positive, and the consequence of a false diagnosis is severe: isolation, rehoming decisions, and in some cases euthanasia. Confirmation by PCR or a second assay format is the standard of care before a lifelong diagnosis is communicated.

## Kittens and the retest rule

Kittens can be antigen positive from maternal exposure or from transient infection, and some clear the virus. The accepted approach is to retest after a defined interval rather than declare a permanent status at first test.

## Why vaccination status is less of an issue than it used to be

FeLV vaccines do not produce a positive antigen result — they are not antigen tests — but vaccination does complicate antibody-based interpretation, which is one reason antigen testing became the standard. Where vaccination is widespread, the important question is not "was this cat vaccinated" but "is this cat antigenaemic now, and does it remain so".

## Clinical management after a confirmed positive

- **Progressive infection:** monitor for lymphoma, anaemia and immunosuppression; keep indoors; separate from negative cats.
- **Regressive infection:** generally good prognosis; monitor and advise on stress and secondary disease.
- **Co-testing:** FeLV and FIV belong in the same workup; co-infection worsens prognosis.

## Six traps

1. **Diagnosing on one positive antigen in a low-risk cat.** Confirm first.
2. **Missing the regressive outcome.** A negative retest does not always mean never infected.
3. **Declaring a kitten's status permanently.** Retest after the interval.
4. **Using antibody instead of antigen.** Uninformative and vaccine-confounded.
5. **Skipping FIV in the same workup.** Two retroviruses, one sample.
6. **Treating a positive as terminal.** Many FeLV cats live years with monitoring.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** The most valuable content on this page is the four-outcome table, because it converts a binary test into a staged diagnosis — and staging is what changes management.

**Reasoning.** FeLV is often presented as a yes/no result, which is both biologically wrong and clinically harmful: a regressive cat and a progressive cat have entirely different prognoses, and treating them identically either over-restricts a healthy cat or under-monitors a sick one.
