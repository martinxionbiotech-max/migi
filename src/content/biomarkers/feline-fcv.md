## Quick Answer

Feline calicivirus testing splits the same way as every respiratory pathogen: antigen detection answers "is this cat shedding FCV now", antibody answers "has this cat met the virus or the vaccine". Because FCV vaccination is near-universal in owned cats, a positive antibody result carries almost no diagnostic information, while a negative antigen result in an acutely affected cat is the finding that matters — with the caveat that FCV is a heterogeneous virus and a single negative swab does not exclude it.

## What the tests measure

| Test | Sample | Answers | Cannot answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| FCV antigen | Oropharyngeal swab, conjunctival swab | Current viral shedding | Whether the cat is protected |
| FCV antibody | Serum | Prior exposure or vaccination | Whether the cat is infected now |

## Why antibody testing is weak here

FCV vaccines are widely used, and vaccine-induced antibody is not distinguishable from field-virus antibody on a binding assay. In an owned adult cat, a positive antibody result means "this cat has been vaccinated or infected at some point" — which is true of most of the population. The test therefore cannot rule anything in or out on its own.

Where antibody has a legitimate place: catteries and shelters assessing population immunity before an outbreak, and decisions about whether a new arrival needs vaccination. Even there, the interpretation is about risk management, not diagnosis.

## Why antigen testing is the anchor

FCV causes a substantial share of feline upper respiratory disease and, in a minority of cases, a severe systemic form (virulent systemic FCV) with high mortality. Antigen detection in an oropharyngeal swab from an acutely affected cat is the useful test, with three practical conditions:

1. **Sample the right site.** Oropharyngeal or conjunctival swabs; a nasal swab alone may under-sample.
2. **Sample early.** Shedding is highest in the acute phase.
3. **Accept the limitation.** A negative swab in a strongly suspected cat does not exclude FCV — the virus is genetically diverse and detection depends on sample quality and timing.

## The systemic form, and why it matters commercially

Virulent systemic FCV presents with oedema, ulceration, jaundice and high mortality, and it spreads within a clinic or shelter. In an outbreak, rapid antigen detection supports isolation decisions on the same day. This is the clinical scenario where a point-of-care quantitative result changes management most clearly.

| Negative | Positive | Vaccinated adult, mild signs | FCV not excluded; consider FHV and other causes |

## Six traps

1. **Using antibody to diagnose FCV.** Nearly uninformative in a vaccinated population.
2. **Excluding FCV on one negative swab.** Sample site and timing matter.
3. **Assuming vaccine strains never appear in testing.** Documented, but requires history and timing.
4. **Missing the systemic form.** Oedema and jaundice in a cat with respiratory signs is an emergency.
5. **Poor swab technique.** The most common cause of a false negative.
6. **Comparing platforms across visits.** Cut-offs and calibration differ.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** FCV's commercial case is outbreak and systemic-disease triage, not routine respiratory screening, and a vendor should say so.

**Reasoning.** Routine respiratory testing is a low-value use: most affected cats are treated symptomatically regardless of result, and the owner often declines the test. The systemic form is different — it is an emergency where a same-day answer changes isolation, staff protocols and prognosis discussions, and where the hospital's own liability is at stake. A vendor that positions the assay for systemic-disease triage is selling into a decision that is already being made under time pressure, which is where point-of-care testing has a genuine advantage over sending a swab away.
