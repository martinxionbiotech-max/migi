## Quick Answer

Feline pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity is the pancreas-specific lipase assay for cats, and it is a genuinely better tool than catalytic lipase activity — which in cats is particularly unreliable. It is also a test whose main clinical risk is over-reading: feline pancreatitis is often mild, chronic and clinically quiet, so a modestly elevated fPL in a cat with non-specific signs is usually a prompt to look further, not a diagnosis. The useful question is not "is fPL high" but "does this cat's clinical picture, imaging and fPL agree?"

## What fPL measures

Feline PLI is an immunoassay against pancreatic lipase protein. Compared with catalytic lipase activity it is far more pancreas-specific, which matters in cats because activity assays are affected by renal clearance and by non-pancreatic sources. Compared with canine PLI, the feline literature is thinner, cut-offs differ, and the correlation between fPL and histological pancreatitis is imperfect. All three facts belong on the page.

## Why cats need a different approach from dogs

| Feature | Dog | Cat |
|---|---|---|
| Typical presentation | Vomiting, abdominal pain, anorexia | Anorexia, lethargy, jaundice; often non-specific |
| Clinical detection | Usually symptomatic | Frequently subclinical or masked by concurrent disease |
| Common comorbidity | — | Cholangitis, inflammatory bowel disease, hepatic lipidosis |
| Risk of over-reading a mild elevation | Moderate | High |

The comorbidity row is the one that changes interpretation. In cats, pancreatitis is commonly part of a triad of disease involving the pancreas, biliary tree and intestine. A mildly elevated fPL in a cat with cholangitis may reflect the shared inflammatory process rather than a primary pancreatic event, and treating it as primary pancreatitis leads to the wrong management plan.

Cats tolerate hospitalisation poorly; a quantitative trend gives a reason to escalate or de-escalate without relying solely on appetite and attitude, which are subjective and slow.

## Interpretation

| fPL | Clinical picture | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Within interval | Anorexia, lethargy | Pancreatitis unlikely; evaluate liver, gut, metabolic causes |
| Mildly elevated | Non-specific signs | Possible contributor; image, check fSAA, review comorbidity |
| Markedly elevated | Compatible signs | Supports pancreatitis; manage clinically, not numerically |
| Elevated | Known cholangitis/IBD | Consider shared inflammation; treat the whole picture |
| Normal | Strong suspicion | Chronic or focal disease remains possible; repeat and image |

## Pairing with fSAA

The two answer different questions and are more useful together than either alone:

- **fPL** asks whether the pancreas is the source.
- **fSAA** asks how much systemic inflammation is present.

A cat with high fPL and normal fSAA has pancreatic involvement without a systemic inflammatory response; a cat with high fPL and high fSAA has pancreatitis plus systemic inflammation, which changes monitoring intensity and prognosis discussion. A cat with normal fPL and high fSAA has an inflammatory process elsewhere — frequently the biliary tree or intestine — and should be worked up accordingly. This pairing is the single most useful thing a feline panel can offer, and it argues for selling the two assays together rather than as alternatives.

## Six traps

1. **Treating a mild elevation as a diagnosis.** Common in cats with concurrent disease.
2. **Importing canine thresholds.** Different assay, different cut-offs.
3. **Excluding pancreatitis on a normal value.** Chronic and focal forms exist.
4. **Ignoring the biliary and intestinal differential.** Triaditis changes management.
5. **Using fPL as a severity marker.** Clinical status and concurrent disease decide severity.
6. **Trending without precision data.** A small change may be noise.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** The commercially correct framing for fPL is as half of a feline pair — pancreas plus systemic inflammation — not as a stand-alone pancreatitis test.

**Reasoning.** Sold alone, fPL invites the two failure modes above: over-reading a mild elevation and excluding disease on a normal value. Neither is a defect of the assay; both are defects of framing. Sold as a pair with fSAA, the clinician gets two orthogonal pieces of information from one sample, and the interpretation becomes a small matrix rather than a threshold — which is both more clinically useful and, not incidentally, a higher-value test bundle. In feline medicine specifically, where examination findings are thin and hospitalisation is stressful, a two-marker panel that shortens the diagnostic loop is an easier sale than a single marker with an ambiguous result.
