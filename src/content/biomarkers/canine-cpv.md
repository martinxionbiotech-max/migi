## Quick Answer

Parvovirus testing is really two different tests with opposite uses. **Antigen** testing on faeces or a rectal swab answers "is this dog shedding virus now" and is the test that changes triage in a suspect puppy. **Antibody** testing answers "has this dog been exposed or vaccinated" and is useless for diagnosing acute disease in a vaccinated animal. Confusing the two produces the two classic errors: diagnosing infection in a recently vaccinated dog with a positive antibody result, and excluding infection in a dog tested too early or too late in the shedding window.

## What each test measures

| Test | Measured in | Answers | Cannot answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| CPV antigen | Faeces, rectal swab | Current viral shedding | Whether the dog has protective immunity |
| CPV antibody | Serum | Prior exposure or vaccination | Whether the dog is currently infected |

The antibody result in a vaccinated dog is expected to be positive; that is the point of vaccination. It is not evidence of infection, and treating it as such is a common source of unnecessary isolation, owner alarm and cost.

## Why quantitative antigen matters

A qualitative strip reports "positive", collapsing a puppy shedding enormous amounts of virus and a mildly affected dog into one category. A quantitative value gives three things:

1. **Magnitude**, which correlates with shedding and therefore with isolation urgency in a ward or shelter.
2. **Trend**, which supports monitoring through the course of the disease.
3. **Discordance detection**, because a result that does not fit the clinical picture prompts a dilution check rather than an assumption.

## The two windows that decide the result

**Too early.** Virus is shed in faeces before and during clinical signs, but a sample taken at the very beginning of illness or from a dog that has been vomiting without diarrhoea may carry little antigen. A negative result in a strongly suspected case is a reason to repeat in 24–48 hours, not to discharge.

**Too late.** Shedding declines as neutralising antibody appears. A dog presenting late, or after passive antibody administration, may test negative while still being clinically affected.

A third window belongs on any page about this assay: **antigen excess**. In a dog shedding very high titres, a sandwich assay can read falsely low — the hook effect. The confirmation is a dilution series, where the value *rises* on dilution.

## Interpretation in practice

| Antigen | Antibody | Clinical picture | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Positive | Not tested | Haemorrhagic diarrhoea, unvaccinated puppy | Treat as parvovirus; isolate |
| Negative | Not tested | Strong suspicion | Repeat in 24–48 h; consider hook effect if severe |
| Negative | Positive | Vaccinated adult, mild signs | Parvovirus less likely; look for other causes |
| Positive | Positive | Vaccinated dog | Possible vaccine-strain shedding or infection; interpret with history |
| Not tested | Positive | Healthy breeding animal | Immune; no action |

## Outbreak and shelter use

In a shelter, the useful pattern is antigen testing at intake for at-risk animals plus isolation on positives, rather than antibody screening. Antibody testing is valuable in a different setting — assessing whether a population is immune before an outbreak, or deciding whether a vaccinated animal needs boosters — but it does not identify shedders.

## Six traps

1. **Using antibody to diagnose acute infection.** The most common and most expensive error.
2. **Excluding parvovirus on a single early negative.** Repeat.
3. **Missing the hook effect in severely affected dogs.** Dilute.
4. **Testing a faecal swab stored improperly.** Antigen degrades; state storage limits.
5. **Ignoring vaccination history.** Changes antibody interpretation entirely.
6. **Assuming a positive antigen means vaccine strain in a recently vaccinated dog.** Possible, but requires history and timing — not an assumption.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** The commercially strongest framing is not "parvovirus test" but "antigen for triage, antibody for immunity" — and a vendor should publish both, because the pair answers the questions shelters and breeders actually ask.

**Reasoning.** Single-antigen positioning puts the product in direct competition with every strip on the market, where the decision reduces to price. The pair positioning changes the conversation: antigen tells the clinic who to isolate today, antibody tells the breeder whether the litter is protected. Those are different buyers with different budgets, and a vendor supplying both becomes the reference point for the disease rather than a line item.
