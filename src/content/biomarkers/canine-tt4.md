## Quick Answer

Total T4 is the entry point to canine thyroid testing, not the answer. It is cheap, widely available, and easily misread: a low total T4 occurs in genuine hypothyroidism, in euthyroid-sick dogs, and in dogs on several common drugs, and a normal total T4 does not exclude disease in every case. The defensible position is that T4 must be interpreted with TSH, clinical signs, and a review of concurrent illness and medication — and that a diagnosis of hypothyroidism should not be made on T4 alone.

## What it measures

Total thyroxine is the sum of protein-bound and free hormone in circulation. Only the free fraction is biologically active, and binding proteins are affected by illness and drugs — which is the mechanism behind most misinterpretation. Total T4 therefore reflects the thyroid gland's output *and* the binding environment, and the two cannot be separated from a single number.

## The three ways a total T4 misleads

| Pattern | Common interpretation | Reality |
|---|---|---|
| Low T4 | Hypothyroidism | Also euthyroid-sick syndrome, glucocorticoids, phenobarbital, sulfonamides, some breeds |
| Low-normal T4 with high TSH | Uncertain | Early or subclinical disease; requires clinical correlation |
| Normal T4 with compatible signs | Not hypothyroid | Possible in early disease, or with altered binding |

The first row accounts for most over-diagnosis. A dog with a serious non-thyroidal illness can have a T4 in the hypothyroid range and recover normal thyroid function after the illness resolves.

## Why quantitative and why pairing with TSH

| Question | T4 alone | T4 + TSH |
|---|---|---|
| Is thyroid output low? | Suggests, ambiguous | Low T4 with high TSH is far stronger evidence |
| Is this non-thyroidal illness? | Not distinguishable | Low T4 with normal TSH suggests illness, not thyroid failure |
| Is supplementation adequate? | Partly | Trend with clinical response |

The pairing is what turns a suggestive number into a defensible diagnosis. A low T4 with a normal or low TSH is the classic euthyroid-sick pattern; a low T4 with a clearly elevated TSH in a dog with compatible signs is a much stronger case for treatment.

## Interpretation matrix

| T4 | TSH | Clinical picture | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | High | Compatible signs | Supports hypothyroidism; consider treatment trial with monitoring |
| Low | Normal | Compatible signs | Euthyroid-sick versus early disease; treat the other illness and retest |
| Low | Normal | Non-thyroidal illness, on glucocorticoids or phenobarbital | Do not diagnose; reassess after resolution or drug change |
| Normal | High | Compatible signs | Unusual; consider assay interference, recent supplementation, or non-thyroidal illness |
| Normal | Normal | Compatible signs | Hypothyroidism unlikely; look elsewhere |

## Monitoring supplementation

Two practical points that are frequently mishandled:

1. **Consistent sampling time relative to the dose.** T4 varies through the dosing interval; comparing values drawn at different intervals produces apparent changes that are pharmacokinetic, not clinical.
2. **Treat the dog, not the number.** Clinical response, body weight, activity and skin should drive dose adjustment; a T4 inside the reference interval with no clinical improvement suggests another diagnosis or a dosing problem.

## Six traps

1. **Diagnosing on a low T4 alone.** The single most common error in veterinary endocrinology.
2. **Ignoring drugs.** Glucocorticoids and phenobarbital lower T4; note this explicitly.
3. **Testing during acute illness.** Postpone unless the question is urgent.
4. **Ignoring breed.** Sighthounds run low physiologically.
5. **Comparing sampling times across visits.** Produces false trends.
6. **Using total T4 where free T4 is needed.** Binding abnormalities are the reason free T4 exists.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** A thyroid panel page is more valuable when it tells the clinician when *not* to test, and the strongest differentiator available here is a clear "do not diagnose on this value alone" statement.

**Reasoning.** Hypothyroidism is one of the most over-diagnosed conditions in small animal practice, and the diagnostic harm is not theoretical: a dog started on levothyroxine for a euthyroid-sick episode may never come off it, while the actual disease goes untreated. A vendor that publishes the interpretation matrix — including the low-T4/normal-TSH row — is doing something commercially counter-intuitive but strategically sound: it makes the test harder to misuse, which reduces the number of cases where the assay is blamed for a clinical misjudgement. Diagnostic companies that sell endocrine tests are, in effect, selling judgement; the vendors that supply judgement explicitly are the ones clinicians keep using.
