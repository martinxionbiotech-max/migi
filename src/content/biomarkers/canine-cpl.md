## Quick Answer

Canine pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity measures the lipase enzyme specifically of pancreatic origin, which is what separates it from total serum lipase activity: activity assays detect lipase from pancreas, stomach and other sources, so they can be elevated in animals whose pancreas is normal. That specificity is the reason cPL is the test to reach for when pancreatitis is on the differential — and the reason a single result still cannot make the diagnosis on its own.

## What cPL measures, and what it does not

Pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity is an immunoassay against the pancreatic lipase protein. It is more pancreas-specific than catalytic lipase activity, and it is not a measure of pancreatic *function* — it indicates leakage of pancreatic enzyme into circulation, which happens with inflammation and, to a lesser degree, with pancreatic damage of other causes.

Three things follow, and each is clinically important:

1. **Elevation supports pancreatitis; it does not define it.** The diagnosis is a composite of compatible clinical signs, cPL, and imaging. A single elevated value in a vomiting dog with no other supporting evidence has a broad differential.
2. **Normal does not exclude it.** Mild, chronic, or localized disease can produce values inside the reference interval.
3. **It is not a severity score.** A high cPL does not reliably predict outcome; clinical status and organ dysfunction do.

## Why quantitative rather than a positive/negative strip

| Clinical question | Strip | Quantitative cPL |
|---|---|---|
| Is the pancreas involved? | Above/below one cut-off | Value relative to platform interval |
| How strong is the signal? | Not available | Magnitude, with the caveat above |
| Is it resolving? | Only after dropping below cut-off | Change between samples against imprecision |
| Is it consistent with the imaging? | Not assessable | Discordance becomes visible and actionable |

The last row matters more than it first appears. When cPL and ultrasound disagree — a high value with an unremarkable pancreas, or a normal value with an abnormal one — the clinician learns something that neither test alone provides.

## Interpretation that holds up in practice

| cPL | Clinical picture | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Within interval | Vomiting, abdominal pain | Pancreatitis unlikely but not excluded; look elsewhere |
| Mildly elevated | Compatible signs | Possible; image and re-check rather than treat as confirmed |
| Markedly elevated | Compatible signs | Supports pancreatitis; assess severity by clinical and biochemical status, not by the number |
| Markedly elevated | No compatible signs | Look for another explanation — gastrointestinal, hepatic, renal, or neoplastic disease can raise it |
| Normal | Strong clinical suspicion | Consider chronic or focal disease; repeat and image |

## Confounders and secondary elevations

cPL is pancreas-specific but not disease-specific. Elevations are reported with:

- gastrointestinal disease (the pancreas and gut share inflammation pathways)
- azotaemia (reduced clearance affects several analytes)
- hepatic disease
- post-operative and post-anaesthetic states
- some neoplastic conditions

This is why the test is best used as one component of a diagnostic composite rather than as a stand-alone rule-in.

## Monitoring

Serial cPL is useful, but slower than acute-phase proteins: values can remain elevated for days after clinical improvement. A falling trend supports resolution; a flat or rising trend in a clinically worsening dog supports complication ( abscess, pseudocyst, or concurrent disease). Do not expect the value to normalise before the patient does.

## Six traps

1. **Importing decision thresholds from another assay.** The most common and most consequential error.
2. **Diagnosing pancreatitis on cPL alone.** The diagnosis is a composite.
3. **Excluding pancreatitis on a normal value.** Mild and chronic forms exist.
4. **Using cPL as a severity marker.** Clinical status and organ function decide severity.
5. **Expecting rapid normalisation.** It lags clinical improvement.
6. **Comparing across platforms mid-case.** Proportional bias makes the trend uninterpretable.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** The commercially useful claim for cPL is not "sensitive detection of pancreatitis" — it is "resolves the discordant case", and a vendor should publish the discordance-handling guidance that supports it.

**Reasoning.** Every vendor claims sensitivity; buyers have learned to discount the word. What clinicians actually struggle with is the case where the number and the ultrasound disagree, and the case where a mildly elevated value arrives in a dog that looks well. Those are interpretation problems, not analytical ones, and they can be addressed with a decision table at zero experimental cost. A vendor that publishes the table is effectively supplying the clinical judgement that its own assay cannot supply — which is the most defensible form of differentiation available to a diagnostic company, because it cannot be copied by adding a feature.
