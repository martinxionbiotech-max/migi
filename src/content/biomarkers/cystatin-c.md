## Quick Answer

Cystatin C is a low-molecular-weight protein that is filtered freely by the glomerulus and largely reabsorbed by the tubules, which makes its plasma concentration a marker of filtration that is less dependent on muscle mass than creatinine. In dogs and cats the evidence base is thinner than for creatinine or SDMA, and the honest position is that cystatin C is a **supporting** marker in cases where the other two disagree — not a replacement for either, and not yet a stand-alone staging variable.

## What it measures

Cystatin C is produced at a relatively constant rate by nucleated cells, handled by glomerular filtration, and catabolised in the proximal tubule. Its concentration therefore rises when filtration falls. Because production is not tied to muscle mass, it avoids the main confounder of creatinine in the two populations where creatinine is least reliable: the heavily muscled dog and the muscle-wasted cat.

That advantage is real but narrower than marketing usually implies. Cystatin C is not a measure of tubular function, not a marker of damage, and not independent of all non-renal influences — thyroid status, glucocorticoid therapy and body composition have all been reported to affect it in mammals.

## Why a third filtration marker at all

| Marker | Strength | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Creatinine | Universally available, well understood | Muscle-mass dependent; insensitive until substantial filtration loss |
| SDMA | Less muscle-mass dependent; more validated than cystatin C in veterinary use | Not a damage marker; interpretation still requires context |
| Cystatin C | Independent of muscle mass; orthogonal to creatinine | Thinner veterinary validation; platform-dependent intervals |

The clinical value is in the disagreement. A thin cat with a high creatinine and a normal cystatin C is a different animal from a thin cat with both high — one has a muscle-mass-independent filtration marker that is reassuring, the other does not. The clinician who has only creatinine cannot make that distinction. That is the case for keeping a third marker on the menu; it is not a case for replacing the first two.

## Interpretation in practice

| Result pattern | Suggested reading |
|---|---|
| Creatinine normal, cystatin C normal | Filtration likely preserved; no reassurance about damage or proteinuria |
| Creatinine high, cystatin C high | Consistent with reduced filtration; assess hydration, urinalysis, blood pressure |
| Creatinine high, cystatin C normal | Muscle-mass effect or extrarenal creatinine influence possible; re-check after rehydration |
| Creatinine normal, cystatin C high | Discordant; repeat, review hydration and thyroid status, consider early filtration loss |
| Both rising over months | Trend supports progressive decline; adjust monitoring interval |

The table is a reasoning aid, not an algorithm. Every row requires hydration status, urinalysis, and — where available — blood pressure, because none of the three filtration markers measures the things that most change management in renal disease: proteinuria, concentrating ability, infection and hypertension.

## Six traps

1. **Treating cystatin C as a superior creatinine.** It is an orthogonal marker, not a better one.
2. **Using a combined canine/feline interval.** Species differences are not resolved by averaging.
3. **Excluding renal disease on a normal value.** Filtration markers say nothing about tubular injury or protein loss.
4. **Ignoring thyroid and glucocorticoid status.** Both have been reported to influence cystatin C in mammals.
5. **Trending without platform precision data.** A small change may be noise.
6. **Comparing platforms.** Calibration differs; a value from a reference laboratory is not interchangeable.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** Cystatin C should be marketed as a **disagreement resolver**, in exactly the way SDMA is, and its page should say plainly that the veterinary evidence base is thinner than for creatinine or SDMA.

**Reasoning.** The temptation is to present cystatin C as the most sophisticated marker on the menu — it is newer, it sounds advanced, and it has an elegant physiological rationale. But buyers in this category include laboratory directors who know the veterinary literature, and a claim that outruns the evidence is the fastest way to lose them. The narrower claim — "useful when creatinine and the clinical picture disagree, especially in the very muscular or very wasted patient" — is defensible, teachable, and survives contact with difficult cases. It also creates a legitimate reason to run three markers rather than one, which is a better commercial argument than replacement, because replacement invites a comparison that cystatin C does not win.
